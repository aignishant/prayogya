## One-line answer

Twelve invented notifications, each carrying what a caller actually said and a hand-written
`expected` block saying what the desk must conclude — four fast-tracks and eight deficiencies, one
per way a notification can fail.

## The idea

Listen to a first-notice-of-loss call and the striking thing is how little of it is fields. Someone
rings because water is coming through a ceiling, and what they say is: *the flexible hose under the
sink let go overnight, the plumber's been, no one's hurt.* Three facts, one reassurance and no
structure at all. The person taking the call turns that into a form while the caller is still
talking, and the form is what the rest of the insurer sees.

The form has holes in it, and this is the part people building claims software get wrong first. A
caller who does not know what day it happened does not become a caller who knows; they become a
notification with no date of loss. A caller ringing from a neighbour's phone that cuts out becomes
a notification with no contact. **The record has to be able to hold an incomplete call**, because
the desk's actual job is noticing what is missing and asking for it — and a record type that
refused to hold a gap would push the failure back to the one moment when nothing can be done about
it.

So the queue is twelve of those, and every one of them is invented.

There is a second thing in the file, and it needs a name because it is doing something unusual.
Each notification carries an `expected` block: what the desk should conclude, written by hand,
before any code existed. That is an **answer key** — this project's declared correct outcome, used
by the tests today and by the evalset on day 18. It sits in the same file as the questions so the
two cannot drift apart, and the desk itself must never read it. `PROJECT.md` promised twelve
notifications, four fast-tracked and eight letters that each name a real missing field; this file
is where that promise stops being a sentence.

**Line by line:**

- `"_synthetic"` again, first, and this one says something the policy file's does not: **the
  `expected` block "is never produced by a model."** That sentence is the difference between a
  fixture set and a recording of a system's own output, and a project that loses it has an eval
  suite that tests whether the system still agrees with itself.
- `"id": "FNOL-4471"` — first notice of loss, numbered. The ids run to 4482, so there are twelve,
  and they are the string that will appear in every log line, every claim file and every letter.
- `"policy_number"` — the join back to the store. `FNOL-4476` quotes **SYN-POL-9999**, which does
  not exist, because a caller reading a number off an old renewal letter is an ordinary Tuesday and
  the desk needs a letter for it.
- `"reported_at"` with a `Z`, and `"date_of_loss"` without a time — **two different kinds of
  moment.** When the call happened is a timestamp the system knows precisely. When the loss
  happened is a date a person is recalling, and pretending to know the minute would be inventing
  precision. FNOL-4473's is `null`, because the caller genuinely does not know.
- `"description"` — free text, in the caller's register, with the ums taken out. Every one of the
  twelve is written to be classifiable by a person reading it and none of them names its peril in
  the words the store uses: nobody says "escape of water", they say the hose let go. That gap is
  exactly the work day 7 hands to a model.
- **FNOL-4471 ends "No one hurt."** That sentence is in the fixture on purpose and it is the single
  most important string in this file. Part 3.2 is about what it does to anything that decides by
  scanning for words.
- `"photo_ref": "PH-9001"` and `"photo_ref": null` — a reference to a photograph, not a photograph.
  The desk decides on the *presence* of evidence, not its content, which keeps this project to text
  and keeps its boundary simple. FNOL-4475 has none and does not need one, because a theft has
  nothing left to photograph; FNOL-4477 has none and does need one.
- `"contact_ref": null` on FNOL-4482 — the caller rang from a neighbour's phone and the line went.
  A deficiency letter that cannot be sent anywhere is a genuine intake problem, and it is here so
  the desk has to have an answer for it.
- `"estimate": 4800` on FNOL-4481 — above SYN-POL-1002's `fast_track_limit` of 2500, which is what
  makes it a deficiency. Note that the estimate is the **caller's** figure, spoken on the phone,
  and the desk is treating it as a fact. That is a real assumption in real intake systems and it is
  worth knowing you have made it.
- `"expected": { "injury": …, "outcome": …, "loss_type": …, "reason": … }` — four declared facts
  per notification. `outcome` and `reason` are the verdict; `loss_type` and `injury` are the two
  things that **require reading a sentence**, supplied here so that the rules can be tested today
  without a model in the loop.
- **`"reason"` is `null` on exactly the fast-tracks and a code on exactly the deficiencies**, and
  the eight codes are eight different ones: a missing date, a missing contact, an unknown policy, a
  policy not in force, a peril not covered, a missing photograph, an injury, and an estimate over
  the limit. One notification per way of failing, so a rule that stops working takes exactly one
  test with it.
- **Four `fast-track` and eight `deficiency`** — the numbers `PROJECT.md` committed to on day 0.
  Part 3.1 asserts them.

Read the queue and decide every one of them with the rules from part 2.1:

```bash

from claims_desk.domain import assess
s = Store(); e = s.expectations()
```

**Line by line:**

- **Twelve lines, four fast-tracks, eight deficiencies, eight distinct reasons.** This is the
  `Done when` paragraph of `PROJECT.md`, minus the letters, running for the first time on day 2.
- The four fast-tracks are two water and storm claims on home policies, a shed theft and a
  windscreen — deliberately across three products, so nothing here works by accident of a single
  product's rules.
- `missing_date_of_loss` on FNOL-4473 comes out even though that notification also has a perfectly
  good policy and photograph. **The first rule that fails is the one the letter names**, and part
  2.1 is where that ordering is chosen.
- Nothing in this run touched a model, a network or a key. Every one of those verdicts is a field
  comparison, and the two facts that could not be compared — the loss type and the injury — were
  read from the answer key rather than guessed at.

## When it breaks

A notification whose `expected` block disagrees with the rules is caught by the test in part 3.1,
and this is what it looks like when it happens. During the writing of this day, `assess` decided
injury for itself by scanning the description, and the very first notification went the wrong way:

```
    def test_the_rules_reproduce_every_declared_outcome(store: Store) -> None:
        expected = store.expectations()
        for notification in store.notifications():
            declared = expected[notification.id]
            policy = store.policy(notification.policy_number)
            outcome, reason = assess(notification, policy, declared["loss_type"])
>           assert outcome == declared["outcome"], notification.id
E           AssertionError: FNOL-4471
E           assert <Outcome.DEFI... 'deficiency'> == 'fast-track'
E             
E             - fast-track
E             + deficiency
```

Verbatim, from this project. The fixture said fast-track, the code said deficiency, and the code
was wrong for a reason nobody would guess from the diff: `No one hurt.` contains the word `hurt`.
The smallest fix was not a better word list — it was to stop `assess` reading English at all and
take the injury as a given, which is the signature it has in part 2.1. Part 3.2 is that failure,
in full.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same twelve cases, plus a generator that produces a few
thousand more by permuting the fields, because hand-written fixtures cover the cases you thought
of. What they would keep by hand is exactly this set: **the twelve that each pin one rule**, with
their expectations written by a person. Generated data tells you the system does not crash;
hand-written data with a declared answer is the only kind that tells you it is right.

**What degrades at scale.** Twelve notifications is a morning's queue at a small branch. A real
intake desk takes several hundred a day, and the proportion that arrive incomplete is not small —
missing dates and unreachable callers are the ordinary case, not the exception. Every deficiency
here is a letter, and eight letters out of twelve is a system whose main output is asking for more
information. Building the fixture set two-thirds deficient is a decision about what this desk
actually is.

**The review comment.** *"The expected outcomes in this fixture were generated by running the
current code. Delete them and write them by hand — an answer key produced by the system under test
only proves the system has not changed."*

**The interview question.** *"Your test fixtures carry the expected answers alongside the inputs.
What breaks if those answers were ever produced by the system, and how would you tell from the
repository whether they had been?"*
