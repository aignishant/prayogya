## One-line answer

A rule that decides by looking for words called a claim an injury because the caller said "No one
hurt" — and the fix was not a better word list, it was to stop the rules reading English at all.

## The idea

A supervisor is training a new starter on the intake phones and gives them a shortcut: *if they
mention anyone being hurt, it doesn't go on the fast track — it goes to an adjuster.* Good advice,
and for a week it works.

Then a call comes in about a burst hose, and the caller finishes with the thing people always
finish with: *plumber's been, no one hurt.* The new starter hears the word, sends it to an
adjuster, and a claim that should have been settled in a day sits in a queue for a fortnight
because the policyholder said the reassuring thing.

Nobody would call that a bug in the new starter. It is a bug in the shortcut. The instruction was
"if they mention anyone being hurt", and what got implemented was "if the word appears" — which are
the same rule right up until somebody says the word in order to deny it.

This part is that failure, in this project's own code, on this project's own data, and it is here
because it is the reason the desk has a model in it at all. Every other rule the desk applies —
was the policy in force, is this peril covered, is the estimate over the limit — is a comparison
between fields. Two are not: **which peril this is**, and **whether anyone was hurt**. Both require
reading a sentence a person spoke, and this part is where that stops looking like a small extra
condition and starts looking like a different kind of problem.

## The mechanism

The stand-in, run against the twelve notifications, with its answer compared to what the fixture
declares:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk.store import Store
from claims_desk.domain import mentions_injury_keyword_scan as scan
s = Store(); e = s.expectations()
for n in s.notifications():
    said = scan(n.description); truth = e[n.id]['injury']
    if said != truth:
        print(f'{n.id}: scan says injury={said}, the notification says injury={truth}')
        print(f'   \"...{n.description[-40:]}\"')
"
```

```
FNOL-4471: scan says injury=True, the notification says injury=False
   "...ber has been and capped it. No one hurt."
```

**Line by line:**

- `mentions_injury_keyword_scan as scan` — imported under a short name here and named in full in
  the module, where the capitalised `STAND-IN` in its docstring is the first thing a reader sees.
  The long name is deliberate: nothing should be able to call this thinking it is the classifier.
- `said = scan(n.description); truth = e[n.id]['injury']` — the stand-in's answer against the
  declared fact from part 1.2's answer key. **Two sources, one of which was written by a person who
  read the sentence.**
- `if said != truth:` — print only the disagreements. Eleven of the twelve agree, which is exactly
  what makes this dangerous: a rule that is right 92% of the time on your own fixtures looks like a
  rule that works.
- **One line of output, and it is the first notification in the queue.** FNOL-4471 is a burst
  flexible hose, £900, a photograph, an in-force policy — the most fast-trackable claim in the set
  — and the scan sends it to an adjuster.
- `"...ber has been and capped it. No one hurt."` — the last forty characters, which is all it
  takes. The word `hurt` is in the sentence. The sentence says the opposite.

Now the fix everybody reaches for first: patch the word list with the negation.

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk.domain import mentions_injury_keyword_scan as scan
NEGATIONS = ('no one hurt', 'nobody hurt')
def patched(text):
    lowered = text.lower()
    if any(n in lowered for n in NEGATIONS):
        return False
    return scan(text)
for line in ('Plumber has been and capped it. No one hurt.',
             'Nobody was injured, the dog was in the garden.',
             'No injuries to report.',
             'The caller was not hurt but the neighbour went to hospital.'):
    print(f'{patched(line)!s:<5} {line}')
"
```

```
False Plumber has been and capped it. No one hurt.
True  Nobody was injured, the dog was in the garden.
True  No injuries to report.
True  The caller was not hurt but the neighbour went to hospital.
```

**Line by line:**

- `NEGATIONS = ('no one hurt', 'nobody hurt')` — two phrasings, chosen because they are the ones
  that came to mind. That is the method, and it is the problem with the method.
- **Line one is fixed.** The exact sentence from the fixture now comes out `False`, and if the only
  test were the twelve notifications in this repository, the patch would be green and the work
  would look done.
- **Line two, `Nobody was injured`, comes out `True`.** The negation list has `nobody hurt` and the
  sentence says `nobody was injured` — one word apart, and the scan is wrong again.
- **Line three, `No injuries to report`, comes out `True`.** A phrasing so common it appears on
  forms.
- **Line four is the interesting one.** `The caller was not hurt but the neighbour went to hospital`
  comes out `True`, which is **the right answer** — somebody did go to hospital — and it is right
  for the wrong reason: the scan matched `hospital`, and it would have matched it equally in *"the
  hospital across the road was not involved"*. A rule that is accidentally right is not a rule you
  can rely on, and it is much harder to find than one that is wrong.
- **Every one of those four sentences would be an ordinary thing to say on a claims call.** The
  patch bought one fixture and no ground.

## When it breaks

Before the fix, `assess` scanned the description itself, and the fixture set caught it on the first
notification:

```
    def test_the_rules_reproduce_every_declared_outcome(store: Store) -> None:
        expected = store.expectations()
        for notification in store.notifications():
            declared = expected[notification.id]
            policy = store.policy(notification.policy_number)
            outcome, reason = assess(notification, policy, declared["loss_type"])
>           assert outcome == declared["outcome"], notification.id
E           AssertionError: FNOL-4471
E           assert <Outcome.DEFI... 'deficiency'> == 'fast-track'
E             
E             - fast-track
E             + deficiency
```

Verbatim, from this project, on the day it was written. The fixture said fast-track, the rules said
deficiency, and the diff gives no clue why: nothing in `assess` looks wrong, and nothing in the
notification looks unusual. It took printing the description to see it.

The smallest fix was **not** a longer word list. It was to change `assess`'s signature so that it
takes the injury as a fact — `assess(notification, policy, loss_type, injury_reported)` — and to
rename the scanner `mentions_injury_keyword_scan`, with a docstring that says it decides nothing.
The rules went back to comparing fields, which is the thing they are good at, and the question that
needs a reader of English became a parameter waiting for one. Day 7 is where it gets filled in.

And the stand-in stayed in the file, wrong, with a test that asserts it is wrong:
`test_the_keyword_scan_is_still_wrong_about_a_real_notification`. **Deleting it would have hidden
the reason the model exists.**

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** They would not write the scan at all — they would write the
signature with the parameter and leave it unfilled until there is something honest to fill it with.
What they would fight for in review is the naming: a helper called `mentions_injury` gets used, and
one called `mentions_injury_keyword_scan` with `STAND-IN` in its docstring gets questioned. The
other habit worth copying is the inverted test. A known-broken component with no test around it
becomes load-bearing by accident, one call site at a time.

**What degrades at scale.** Eleven right out of twelve is 92%, and on a desk taking several hundred
notifications a day that is dozens of claims a week routed wrongly — most of them, on this
evidence, fast-trackable claims sent to an adjuster because the caller offered a reassurance. The
cost is not visible as errors. It is visible as an adjuster queue that is longer than it should be
and a fast-track rate that nobody can explain, which is precisely the kind of number that gets
attributed to "the mix of claims this quarter". A rule that fails silently in the direction of more
work is the hardest kind to find.

**The review comment.** *"This adds `not_hurt`, `no injuries` and `nobody was hurt` to the negation
list. Every one of those is a sentence somebody thought of on a Tuesday. What is the test that
tells us the list is finished?"*

**The interview question.** *"You have a rule that looks for keywords in free text and it is right
92% of the time on your fixtures. Convince me that is not good enough — and then tell me what you
would replace it with and how you would know the replacement is better."*
