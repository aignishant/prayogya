## One-line answer

Exactly one event in this desk wakes somebody on its own — the boundary refusing a write, because
nothing is being recorded and every further claim is processed into nothing — and everything else is a
ticket for the morning or a number on a chart, with two events promoted to a page only when a quarter
of the queue is affected.

## The idea

There is a telephone on a wall in every insurer's operations room, and the only thing that matters
about it is how often it rings for nothing.

A rota that gets woken twice a week for something that could have waited until nine stops reading the
alert before acknowledging it. That is not a failure of discipline; it is what anybody does after the
twentieth false alarm, and it is entirely predictable from the alerting rules. The cost is paid on the
one night the page is real, when somebody acknowledges it from bed, goes back to sleep, and finds out
in the morning.

So the question is not "which events are serious". Every event this desk logs is serious to somebody —
a held claim matters to a policyholder, a refused draft matters to whoever wrote the house style, an
escalation matters to the handler who picks it up. The question is **whether there is something a
person can do now, that waiting makes worse.** That is a much narrower test, and applied honestly to
this desk it catches one thing.

Work through the candidates, because the reasoning is the content here and the table is just where it
ends up.

**A claim the desk could not classify.** `desk.provider_unavailable` — day 15's escalation. It is the
system working exactly as designed: rather than fabricate a classification, it marked the claim pending
with a reason and moved on. A handler picks it up. There is nothing to do at three in the morning that
is better than what already happened, so this is a number.

**A claim held for approval.** `desk.awaiting_approval` — day 16's gate. A person *must* look at this,
which makes it tempting. But the morning is soon enough; that is what the gate is for. A ticket.

**An instruction-shaped claim note.** `security.instruction_shaped_input`. Day 16 was explicit that
this is a signal and not a filter, and that the only useful thing to do with it is count it. One is a
curiosity. A number.

**A draft refused for quoting the policyholder.** `hooks.refused_leak` — day 11's guard, working. A
guard firing is good news. A number.

**The boundary refusing a write.** `desk.boundary_refused`. This one is different in kind. The desk is
deciding claims and the boundary is rejecting them, so `run_queue` is producing counts for decisions
that did not land — day 6's failure, which that day caught by reading the tool's answer. There is an
action (stop the queue), waiting makes it worse (every further claim is processed into nothing), and
nobody finds out by any other route. **That is the page.**

Then there are the two that change level with volume. One escalation is the system being honest; a
quarter of the queue escalating is the provider being gone, and somebody should know before the morning
arrives with three hundred unprocessed claims. Same for instruction-shaped notes: one is noise, a
quarter of a morning is somebody working through your intake form deliberately. Thresholds are
**shares of the queue** rather than counts, so that three escalations in twelve is a page and three in
four hundred is a Tuesday.

One more decision, and it is the one people get wrong in the other direction. What level is an event
that no rule mentions? Silence is the tempting answer and it is wrong: an event somebody added last
week and never classified is exactly the thing that should be looked at once. So an unclassified event
is a **ticket**, and the absence of a rule is itself visible.

## The mechanism

The whole module, at `claims_desk/alerts.py`:

```python
"""What wakes a person up, what becomes a ticket, and what is only ever counted.

Every event this desk logs is interesting to somebody. Almost none of it should ring a telephone at
three in the morning, and the difference is not severity — it is **whether there is an action**.

A claim the desk could not classify is a claim a handler picks up in the morning. That is the
system working as designed, and paging for it trains whoever is on call to acknowledge pages
without reading them, which is how a real outage gets missed. A boundary that refuses a write is
different: nothing is recorded, the queue reports counts for decisions that did not land, and every
minute it continues is more claims processed into nothing. Somebody has to know now.

So the rules are data, in one table, with a `why` column that has to be filled in — the same
argument as day 16's threat model, for the same reason. The rule that cannot be justified in one
sentence is the rule that pages somebody for no reason.

Three levels and nothing in between:

- `PAGE` — wake a person. There is something they can do, and waiting makes it worse.
- `TICKET` — somebody reads this in working hours. Real, not urgent.
- `COUNT` — a number on a chart. Interesting in aggregate, never on its own.

What this module does **not** do is send anything. It classifies, and `summarise` reduces one run's
log lines to the decision. Wiring that to a pager is day 19's problem, and keeping the judgement
separate from the delivery is what makes the judgement testable.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections import Counter
from collections.abc import Iterable
from typing import Any

PAGE = "page"
TICKET = "ticket"
COUNT = "count"

#: What each event is worth, and why. The `why` is not documentation: a rule whose justification
#: cannot be written in one sentence is a rule that will page somebody at three in the morning for
#: something they cannot act on.
RULES: tuple[dict[str, str], ...] = (
    {
        "event": "desk.boundary_refused",
        "level": PAGE,
        "why": "nothing is being recorded, and every further claim is processed into nothing",
    },
    {
        "event": "desk.provider_unavailable",
        "level": COUNT,
        "why": "one claim escalated honestly; the outage only matters as a rate, see BURSTS",
    },
    {
        "event": "desk.awaiting_approval",
        "level": TICKET,
        "why": "a person must look at this claim, and the morning is soon enough",
    },
    {
        "event": "security.instruction_shaped_input",
        "level": COUNT,
        "why": "one odd claim note is a curiosity; a burst of them is the thing to look at",
    },
    {
        "event": "desk.letter_failed",
        "level": TICKET,
        "why": "a decision is recorded with no letter sent, which a handler must put right",
    },
    {
        "event": "hooks.refused_leak",
        "level": COUNT,
        "why": "the guard working. A burst means something upstream began leaking the description",
    },
    {
        "event": "workflow.refused_shape",
        "level": COUNT,
        "why": "day 13's schema doing its job on one bad answer; the rate is the signal",
    },
    {
        "event": "hooks.model_error",
        "level": TICKET,
        "why": "a provider error the retry did not absorb, worth a look but not a night",
    },
)

#: Events that are only a page when there are enough of them, and how many is enough. A single
#: escalation is the system being honest; a third of the queue escalating is an outage. The
#: figures are shares of the queue rather than counts, so they survive a queue of another size.
BURSTS: tuple[dict[str, Any], ...] = (
    {
        "event": "desk.provider_unavailable",
        "share": 0.25,
        "why": "a quarter of the queue undecided means the provider is gone, not flaky",
    },
    {
        "event": "security.instruction_shaped_input",
        "share": 0.25,
        "why": "a quarter of a morning carrying instruction-shaped text is not a coincidence",
    },
)

#: The level for an event no rule mentions. `TICKET` and not `COUNT`, deliberately: a new event
#: nobody classified is a thing somebody should look at once, and silence is how an event that
#: deserved a rule goes five releases without getting one.
UNCLASSIFIED = TICKET


def level_for(event: str) -> str:
    """What one event is worth on its own, ignoring how many of them there are."""
    for rule in RULES:
        if rule["event"] == event:
            return rule["level"]
    return UNCLASSIFIED


def summarise(lines: Iterable[dict[str, Any]], claims: int) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Reduce one run's log records to a paging decision.

    `claims` is the size of the queue, because every burst threshold is a share of it. A function
    that took only the lines would have to guess, and a guess here is the difference between paging
    on an outage and paging because the morning was busy.

    Returns the decision, the reasons for it, and the counts it was made from — all three, because a
    page that does not say what it was counting is a page somebody has to reproduce by hand.
    """
    counts = Counter(str(record.get("event", "")) for record in lines)
    reasons: list[str] = []
    worst = COUNT
    for rule in RULES:
        if not counts[rule["event"]]:
            continue
        if rule["level"] == PAGE:
            reasons.append(f"{rule['event']} x{counts[rule['event']]}: {rule['why']}")
        if rule["level"] == TICKET and worst == COUNT:
            worst = TICKET
    for burst in BURSTS:
        seen = counts[str(burst["event"])]
        if claims > 0 and seen / claims >= burst["share"]:
            reasons.append(
                f"{burst['event']} x{seen} of {claims} claims: {burst['why']}"
            )
    return {
        "level": PAGE if reasons else worst,
        "reasons": reasons,
        "counts": dict(counts),
    }
```

**Line by line:**

- **The docstring's third paragraph is the argument and it names the real cost**: paging for a designed
  behaviour *trains whoever is on call to acknowledge pages without reading them*. That is the sentence
  to keep, because everybody agrees with it in the abstract and adds the page anyway.
- `PAGE`, `TICKET`, `COUNT` **as plain strings, three of them.** Not an `IntEnum` with five levels, and
  not `severity=1..5`. A numeric scale invites arguing about whether something is a 2 or a 3, which is
  a discussion with no answer; three named destinations force the only question that matters, which is
  *who receives this and when*.
- `RULES` **is a tuple of dicts with a mandatory `why`** — the same shape as day 16's `THREATS`, for the
  same reason, and the tests assert the same property. A rule nobody can justify in a sentence is the
  one that produces the 3am page for nothing.
- **The `why` for `provider_unavailable` points at `BURSTS`** rather than just saying "not urgent". A
  reader who disagrees with the level needs to know the event is not being ignored, only counted
  differently, or they will add a second rule and page twice.
- `desk.boundary_refused` **is first in the table and is the only `PAGE`.** There is a test asserting
  that list is exactly one event long — not because one is a magic number, but because the list growing
  is the thing worth a conversation, and a test is how the conversation gets started.
- `BURSTS` **uses `share` and not a count.** A threshold of "three escalations" is correct for this
  twelve-claim fixture and wrong the day the queue is four hundred, and the failure is the bad kind:
  it pages more as the system gets busier, which is precisely when nobody can afford to read them.
- `share: 0.25` **with the reasoning in `why`.** Quarter of the queue, not a tenth: a tenth of twelve
  claims is one claim, which would make the burst rule fire on a single escalation and duplicate the
  `COUNT` rule it is meant to refine.
- `UNCLASSIFIED = TICKET` **with the comment explaining the direction of the choice.** The failure this
  defends against is specific: somebody adds `desk.something_new`, nothing classifies it, and it
  silently never reaches anybody. A ticket is a cheap way for the absence to become visible.
- `level_for` **is a linear scan over eight rules.** A dictionary would be faster and would also make
  the table a mapping, losing the `why` column's position beside the level. At eight rows the scan is
  free, and the table is the artefact people read.
- `summarise(lines, claims)` **takes the queue size as an argument** and its docstring says why: every
  burst threshold is a share, so a function that inferred the queue size from the lines would be
  guessing, and the guess is the difference between paging on an outage and paging on a busy morning.
- `Counter(str(record.get("event", "")))` — **tolerant of a line with no `event`.** Log files contain
  lines nobody expected; a paging decision that raises on a malformed record is a paging system that
  goes quiet at the worst moment.
- `worst = COUNT` **and the `TICKET` promotion inside the loop.** This is the bit that was wrong in the
  first draft: the original returned `TICKET` whenever the run contained *any* event, which made a
  perfectly clean queue a ticket. The level now comes from the rules rather than from whether anything
  happened.
- **Reasons are built for pages only**, and each one carries the count and the `why`. That string is
  what a person reads half-awake, so it says what fired, how many times, and why it matters, in one
  line.
- `"counts": dict(counts)` — **the numbers the decision was made from, returned with it.** A page
  without its evidence is one somebody has to reproduce by hand at three in the morning, from logs,
  while deciding whether to wake somebody else.
- **Nothing here sends anything.** `summarise` returns a dictionary. That is what makes every rule in
  this module testable without a pager, an HTTP mock or a queue, and it is why the tests in part 4.1 can
  assert the judgement rather than the plumbing.

The real queue, end to end, decided by this module:

```
level  : ticket
reasons: []
counts : {"span": 108, "hooks.model": 15, "workflow.rules": 8, "workflow.triage": 8,
          "desk.decided": 12, "workflow.house_style": 7, "workflow.correspondence": 7,
          "desk.awaiting_approval": 1, "desk.queue_finished": 1}
```

**Line by line:**

- **`ticket`, not `page`.** Twelve claims, four fast-tracked, seven recorded, one held — and nobody is
  woken up. That is the correct answer and it is the one a system has to be able to give, because an
  alerting configuration that never says "nothing" is one that will not be believed when it says
  something.
- `reasons: []` — **empty, and that is the whole page decision.** The level is `ticket` because of the
  single `desk.awaiting_approval`: a held claim needs somebody in the morning and nobody tonight.
- **`span: 108` is the largest count in the run**, which is worth noticing here rather than in a
  performance section: the instrument is now the most frequent thing in the log. Part 2.2's production
  note is about what to do when that stops being acceptable.
- **Nothing in this list is a `PAGE` rule.** `desk.boundary_refused` does not appear, because every
  write landed.

## When it breaks

Add the rule everybody wants to add. A claim the desk could not classify is a claim that did not get
processed, and surely that is worth knowing about immediately:

```diff
     {
         "event": "desk.provider_unavailable",
-        "level": COUNT,
+        "level": PAGE,
         "why": "a claim went undecided and somebody should know",
     },
```

```
    def test_exactly_one_event_pages_on_its_own() -> None:
        """If everything is a page, nothing is. The bar is: there is an action and waiting is worse."""
        pages = [rule["event"] for rule in alerts.RULES if rule["level"] == alerts.PAGE]
>       assert pages == ["desk.boundary_refused"]
E       AssertionError: assert ['desk.bounda...r_unavailable'] == ['desk.boundary_refused']
E         Left contains one more item: 'desk.provider_unavailable'
```

The test does not say the new rule is wrong. It cannot — that is a judgement, and a test has no
standing to make it. What it says is that **the set of things that wake a person has changed**, and
that change should be a conversation rather than a line in a diff nobody reads closely.

Here is the conversation it forces. With this rule in place, the day the provider has a bad five
minutes, one claim escalates and somebody's telephone rings. They get up, read the log, find a claim
marked pending with a reason, and discover there is nothing to do — day 15 already handled it
correctly. Repeat that a few times and the rota stops reading pages. Then the boundary starts refusing
writes at two in the morning, the real page fires, and it is acknowledged by somebody who has learned
that these are never anything.

**The page you add today is paid for on the night of the page you needed.** The smallest fix is to put
the level back and, if the escalation rate genuinely matters, to tune the `BURSTS` share instead —
which is the mechanism that already exists for exactly this worry.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** These rules as an alerting configuration in whatever the estate
already runs, generated from this table rather than maintained twice — plus two things this module has
no concept of: a **burn rate** against an objective rather than a share of one run, and deduplication,
so the same condition firing on every queue run for an hour is one page and not twelve. They would also
add an on-call runbook link per rule, because the `why` says what it means and the person woken up needs
to know what to type.

**What degrades at scale.** A share-of-the-queue threshold works on twelve claims and on four hundred.
What it cannot do is notice a slow drift: two per cent of claims escalating every day for a month never
crosses a twenty-five per cent threshold and is a real and worsening problem. That needs a rate compared
against its own history rather than against the current run, which is the point at which this module
stops being enough and something with a time-series database starts.

**The review comment.** *"`summarise` is called by nothing in the desk — it is a function the tests
exercise and the running system never reaches. Either call it at the end of `run_queue` and log the
decision, or say in the docstring that it is for whatever reads the log afterwards, because an
alerting module with no call site is one that will be wrong when somebody finally wires it up."*

**The interview question.** *"Give me an event in your system that is serious, that you would not page
for, and your reasoning. Then give me one you would page for, and tell me what the person you woke is
supposed to do in the first five minutes."*
