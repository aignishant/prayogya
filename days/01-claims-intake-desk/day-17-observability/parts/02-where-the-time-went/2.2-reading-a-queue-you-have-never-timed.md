## One-line answer

Sixty-eight seconds for twelve claims, of which **ninety-eight per cent was launching Python
subprocesses**: forty boundary calls at a mean of 1677 milliseconds to connect and 14.8 milliseconds
to answer — a number that existed every day since day 4 and that nobody could have guessed, because
the models, which everybody assumes are the cost, came to seven tenths of a second in total.

## The idea

The most useful thing a measurement does is contradict you.

Ask anybody which part of an agentic claims desk is slow and the answer is the model. It is a network
call to somebody else's computer, it generates text a token at a time, and it is the one component
with a price per request. Every instinct points at it, and the instinct is usually right — in a system
that calls a real provider, the model is most of the wall clock and all of the bill.

This desk runs a scripted stand-in, so its model calls are nearly free, and that changes which answer
is *interesting* rather than which answer is *true*. If the models are not the cost, something else is,
and before today this project had no way to say what. Thirteen days of careful work had produced a
system whose slowest component was unknown — not suspected-and-unconfirmed, but genuinely unknown,
because nothing had ever been timed.

So this part is the first reading of the instrument, and it is worth doing the arithmetic out loud
rather than skipping to the conclusion, because the conclusion is only convincing if you can see where
it came from. Three hundred and two log lines, one trace, a hundred and forty-eight spans. Seven span
names. The question is which of the seven holds the time, and the answer turns out to be the one that
was not there yesterday.

One caveat first, said plainly. These are **this machine's** numbers: Windows, a `uv`-managed
interpreter, an antivirus that inspects every process launch. A Linux container would start that
subprocess faster, possibly several times faster. What does not change is the *shape* — a per-call
process launch against a per-call unit of work measured in milliseconds — and the shape is the finding.
A reader on a faster machine should re-run it and get a different number with the same conclusion.

**Line by line:**

- **Both spans wrap the `await sessions.turn(...)` and nothing else.** Not the agent construction, not
  the state assembly, not the schema validation that happens afterwards in `run_triage`'s `except`
  clause. A span named `triage` that included building an `Agent` object would attribute this desk's
  own set-up to the model.
- **The whole call had to be re-indented**, which is most of the diff's size and is worth noticing
  rather than skipping: wrapping a region in a context manager is a change to the shape of the code,
  and a large diff for a small behaviour change is the normal cost of adding a span.
- `turn_span["events"] = len(events)` **inside the `with`**, on both. The count is not knowable when
  the span opens, and putting it on the span's line means one record answers *how long did this turn
  take and how much came back*, which two separate lines would make somebody join by hand.
- **`correspondence` also records `reason=reason`.** A letter's cost depends on which deficiency it is
  about — `peril_not_covered` produces different prose from `missing_photo_reference` — so the span
  carries the dimension somebody would want to group by.
- **`run_triage`'s span is inside the `try`.** A schema refusal raises `ValidationError` out of
  `sessions.turn`, so the span closes with `ok: false` and `failed: "ValidationError"` and still gets
  logged. That is the case day 13 built and it is exactly the case a trace must not lose.

**Line by line:**

- `trace.start()` **is called once, in `run_queue`, and this is the only place in the desk that starts
  a trace.** One trace for a morning's work; a claim is found within it by its span rather than by an
  id of its own. Starting one per claim would also have been defensible and would make "how long did
  the queue take" the question that needs joining.
- `with trace.span("queue") as queue_span:` **wraps the whole loop**, so the queue span's duration is
  the run. Part 2.2's first check — that the claim spans sum to the queue span — is only possible
  because these two exist at different levels.
- `queue_span.update(counts)` **after the loop**: the outcome tally lands on the queue's own span line,
  so one record carries the duration and the result together.
- **The claim span opens after the `Budget`**, not before. Constructing a budget is bookkeeping, and a
  span that starts a hair earlier would be measuring this desk's object creation.
- **The whole loop body moved one level right**, and the `continue` became an `elif`. That is the
  substantive change in this diff: a `continue` inside a `with` block would exit the block, which is
  correct here — the span would close — but it would skip `claim_span["spent"] = budget.spent` at the
  bottom, so a held claim would have no budget on its line. Restructuring to `if held: ... elif
  verdict.decided: ...` keeps one exit path and one place where the span's last field is set.
- `claim_span["outcome"] = verdict.outcome` **and then again as `PENDING` if the claim is held.** The
  span reports what the queue counted, including the correction day 16's gate makes, so the span lines
  and the final tally cannot disagree.
- `claim_span["held"] = True` — **present only on a held claim**, so `grep '"held": true'` is the query
  that finds them.
- **`log("desk.boundary_refused", ...)` before the `raise`**, with a comment saying why both. The
  traceback reaches whoever ran the queue; the log line reaches whatever is watching. Part 3.1 is about
  why this is the one event in this desk that wakes a person, and until today it existed only as an
  exception — which nothing can page on.
- `claim_span["spent"] = budget.spent` **at the bottom of the `with`**, reached by every path. That is
  what puts day 11's budget count on the same line as the timing, and it is why part 2.2's per-claim
  table has a `spent` column at all.

## When it breaks

The failure here is not in the code. It is in what a reasonable person does with this table, and it is
the most common mistake made immediately after a system is first instrumented: **optimising the biggest
number instead of the biggest number you can do something about.**

Watch how it goes wrong. `boundary.connect` is 67.1 seconds, so cache the connection — keep one client
open for the whole queue and reuse it. The queue drops to about a second. Every test passes. It is
obviously right.

And the thing that was bought on day 3 is quietly gone. A connection per call is why a tool call that
hangs cannot wedge the next claim; why day 15's retry can re-establish a connection rather than reuse a
broken one; why the boundary process holds no accumulated state between claims; and why day 16 could
say that the desk and the boundary are two processes with separate lifetimes rather than one process
and a long-lived channel it depends on. A shared client is shared state, in a queue day 10 made
concurrent, reached from several claims at once.

None of that means the connection cost should stand. It means the fix is a piece of design work with a
failure story — a pool with a size, a health check, a policy for what happens when a connection dies
mid-claim, and a test for the concurrent case — and not a module-level `client` created at import. The
measurement says it is worth doing. It does not say it is easy, and a table of timings is very
persuasive about the first thing and silent about the second.

The smallest honest next step is the one in the checklist: write the `TODO` with the number in it. The
general rule: **a measurement tells you what to work on, never what to do.** The span said where the
time goes; deciding what to do about it needs day 3's reasoning and day 15's, neither of which is in
the trace.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** A connection pool with a bounded size, a health check, and a
per-claim timeout — plus the `boundary.connect` span kept, because a pool turns a fixed 1.7-second
toll into a number that is usually near zero and occasionally is not, and the occasional one is what
you need to see. They would also keep a span round the handshake specifically, since a pool that
silently reconnects on every call looks exactly like a pool that is working.

**What degrades at scale.** Eight thousand notifications a day at forty calls per twelve claims is
roughly twenty-seven thousand boundary calls, and at 1.7 seconds each that is about thirteen hours of
wall clock a day spent starting Python interpreters — for a workload whose actual tool time is about
seven minutes. The number stops being an inefficiency and becomes an impossibility somewhere around a
few hundred notifications a day, which is a small insurer. This is the measurement that decides the
shape of day 19's deployment.

**The review comment.** *"This table is from one run on one machine, and the conclusion is a process
launch cost that is heavily platform-dependent. Before anybody designs a pool around it, run it in the
container day 19 builds and put both numbers in the commit message — because if the container starts
the boundary in 200ms, the right fix might be nothing at all."*

**The interview question.** *"You instrument a service for the first time and find that one span is
ninety-eight per cent of the request. Walk me through what you do next — and tell me what you would
need to know before you change anything."*
