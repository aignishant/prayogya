## One-line answer

A trace with a gap tells you where the time **is not**, and this desk's first trace had a
1863-millisecond `boundary.call` containing nothing — so the question "is the boundary slow?" had no
answer until a span went round the part nobody thought of timing, which turned out to be launching a
Python interpreter.

## The idea

An adjuster's day sheet has a column for travel.

It is there because the first version did not have one. That version recorded the time each visit
started and finished, totalled it, and produced a number that made no sense: eight visits, four hours
of recorded work, ten hours on the road. The missing time was not hidden — it was simply in no column,
and the sheet had no way to say *between the third visit and the fourth, I drove forty minutes*. You
could subtract one visit's end from the next one's start and get the number, but nobody did, because
the sheet looked complete.

That is the failure mode of a trace, and it is worth naming precisely because it does not look like a
failure. Spans are things somebody chose to measure. A region nobody wrapped does not appear as a gap
labelled *unknown*; it appears as **nothing at all**, or — worse — as time attributed to whichever
enclosing span happened to contain it. The trace is green, every number in it is correct, and it is
pointing at the wrong thing.

Here is how that looked on this desk. The first spans were put exactly where you would expect: round
the queue, round each claim, round each agent turn, and round each boundary call. Four span names, all
obviously worth having. And the first trace of one claim said this: the claim took 1865 milliseconds,
of which the boundary call took 1863.

So the boundary is slow. That is the only reading available, and it is a reasonable one — the boundary
reads JSON files off a disk, there is serialisation, there is a protocol. Somebody who believed it
would go and optimise `claims_mcp/tools.py`, which is a week of work on code that is not the problem.

The question the trace cannot answer is: **slow doing what?** `boundary.call` is one span covering two
completely different activities — opening a connection to the boundary, and asking it a question. Day
4 built both and the docstring of `call` has been saying the awkward part out loud since then: *a
connection per call, on purpose and only for now.* Nobody had ever measured what "for now" cost,
because until today nothing in this project measured anything.

The fix is one more span, and then one more inside that. What they found is part 2.2. What this part
is about is the reasoning that got there, because the skill is not adding spans — it is **looking at a
trace and noticing that a number is unexplained**, which requires the habit of asking, of every span,
"what is inside this that I have not named?"

## The mechanism

Start with what the first version produced. One claim, one policy lookup, spans around the claim and
around the boundary call:

```
{"ts": "2026-09-12T05:05:42.959+00:00", "trace": "t-dc50eb3d4e7a", "span": "boundary.call", "event": "boundary.connected", "transport": "stdio", "protocol": "2026-07-28", "server": "claims-boundary"}
{"ts": "2026-09-12T05:05:42.962+00:00", "trace": "t-dc50eb3d4e7a", "event": "boundary.fetch_policy", "number": "SYN-POL-1001", "found": true}
{"ts": "2026-09-12T05:05:43.324+00:00", "trace": "t-dc50eb3d4e7a", "event": "span", "span": "boundary.call", "parent": "claim", "ms": 1863.4, "ok": true, "failed": null, "tool": "fetch_policy", "fields": ["excess", "expiry", "fast_track_limit", "found", "inception", "number", "perils", "product", "status"]}
{"ts": "2026-09-12T05:05:43.325+00:00", "trace": "t-dc50eb3d4e7a", "event": "span", "span": "claim", "parent": null, "ms": 1865.6, "ok": true, "failed": null, "claim": "FNOL-4477"}
```

**Line by line:**

- `"ms": 1863.4` **on `boundary.call`, and 1865.6 on the claim that contains it.** Two milliseconds of
  the claim are the desk's own work. Everything else is in one span with one name.
- **There are only two spans, and the trace is complete by its own lights.** Nothing is missing,
  nothing failed, no line says *unaccounted*. This is what a misleading trace looks like: correct and
  uninformative.
- **Look at the timestamps, though, because they are the clue.** `boundary.connected` is written at
  `42.959` and `boundary.fetch_policy` — the line the *server* writes when it answers — at `42.962`.
  Three milliseconds apart. And the span closes at `43.324`, another 362 milliseconds later. The work
  is over long before the span is, and the only way to see that is by subtracting timestamps by hand,
  which is the adjuster's day sheet exactly.
- **The span started before `42.959` too.** `43.324` minus 1863.4ms puts its start at `41.46`, a second
  and a half before the connection was even reported open. So most of the span is *before* the first
  line inside it, which is the shape of a cost paid during set-up.

That reading is what suggests the next move. Put a span round the connection, and another round the
call itself — `claims_desk/boundary.py`, **a marked diff against the file day 4 printed**:

**Line by line:**

- **`boundary.connect` wraps the `async with`, not the body.** That is the whole point: the span has to
  include the set-up and tear-down of the connection, which is where the suspicion says the time is.
  Wrapping only the inside would have reproduced the original problem one level down.
- **The connect span encloses `yield client`**, so it covers the whole lifetime of the connection
  including the caller's use of it. That is deliberate and it is why `boundary.tool` appears as a
  *child* of `boundary.connect` rather than a sibling — the nesting in the output is a true statement
  about the code.
- `boundary.tool` **wraps exactly one line**: `await client.call_tool(...)`. A span around one
  statement looks excessive and is the most valuable span in the file, because it is the only one that
  measures the thing the name "boundary call" made everybody assume was being measured.
- **`is_error` is checked outside the tool span** and the answer is built outside it too. Both are the
  desk's work, not the boundary's, and a span that includes the caller's parsing is a span that blames
  the wrong process.
- `timing["fields"] = sorted(answer)` — **the span reports which fields came back**, using the mutable
  dictionary part 1.1's `span` yields. Useful on its own, and it is also the field that day 16's
  projection test is about: a `boundary.call` line showing `holder_ref` in `fields` would be the leak
  visible in a log.
- **`fields` and not `keys`.** That name is a scar and part 3.2 is the story: day 1's redactor matches
  `key` as a substring, so a field called `keys` is replaced with `<redacted>` and the line says
  nothing at all.

```
{"ts": "2026-09-12T05:00:50.996+00:00", "trace": "t-c1de696d9398", "event": "span", "span": "boundary.call", "parent": "claim", "ms": 2079.2, "ok": true, "failed": null, "tool": "draft_letter", "fields": ["claim_id", "drafted", "house_style", "needed", "reason", "why"]}
```

**Line by line:**

- **`boundary.tool`: 12.5ms. `boundary.connect`: 2078.9ms.** The boundary is not slow. The boundary
  answers a question in twelve and a half milliseconds. Two whole seconds went on getting a connection
  to it.
- **And `boundary.call` is 2079.2 — three tenths of a millisecond more than the connect it contains.**
  So there is nothing else hiding in there. The trace now accounts for itself, which is the property
  the first version lacked.
- **What is in those two seconds** is `python -m claims_mcp` starting: a fresh interpreter, importing
  `mcp`, `pydantic`, this project's own modules, then a protocol exchange. It is not a mystery once it
  is named, and it was invisible for thirteen days.
- **The nesting makes the claim precise.** `boundary.tool`'s parent is `boundary.connect`, whose parent
  is `boundary.call`. A reader can tell from the `parent` fields alone that the tool call happened
  *inside* a connection, which is what makes "the connection is the cost" a statement about structure
  rather than a guess from two numbers.

## When it breaks

The failure in this part is the first trace, and it is worth stating as a failure in its own right
rather than as a step on the way: **a green, complete, internally consistent trace that supports a
wrong conclusion.**

Reproduce it by taking the two spans out again — the tidying somebody does when three spans round one
call looks like clutter:

```diff
-    with trace.span("boundary.connect", transport="http" if isinstance(where, str) else "stdio"):
-        async with Client(where) as client:
+    async with Client(where) as client:
@@
-            with trace.span("boundary.tool", tool=tool):
-                result = await client.call_tool(tool, arguments)
+            result = await client.call_tool(tool, arguments)
```

Every test in this project still passes — **250 of them**. Nothing in the suite asserts that the
boundary's time is broken down, because until it was, nobody knew there was anything to break down.
And the trace goes back to saying `boundary.call: 1863ms` with nothing inside, which is the number that
sends somebody to optimise the wrong file.

What makes this recoverable is the habit rather than a test. The question to ask of every span is
**"what is inside this that I have not named?"** — and the tell, when the answer is "something", is
almost always visible in the timestamps: a span whose first internal log line is half a second after
its own start has half a second of unnamed work at the front.

The smallest fix is the two spans. The general rule is the one to take from today: **a span is a claim
about where time goes, and a span with unexplained time inside it is a claim that is false in a way
nobody will notice.** Put spans either side of every boundary the code crosses — a process, a network,
a disk — and not merely around the functions that felt important.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** Spans from a library, at the client rather than by hand — most MCP
and HTTP clients will instrument connection set-up, request and response separately if asked, because
the distinction between "connecting" and "calling" is one every client library already knows about.
Hand-rolled spans are for the regions only this codebase understands: a claim, a triage, a letter
loop. The lesson about *where* to put them survives either way.

**What degrades at scale.** Three spans per boundary call is forty calls and a hundred and twenty span
lines for twelve claims. At an intake desk taking eight thousand notifications a day that is close to
eighty thousand span lines, and the `boundary.tool` span — the most valuable one here — is also the
most repetitive, because once the connection cost is fixed it will say 12ms eighty thousand times a
day. The shape that survives is keeping the span and sampling it: every `boundary.call`, one in a
hundred `boundary.tool`, and an aggregate rather than a line for the rest.

**The review comment.** *"`boundary.connect` wrapping the `yield` means its duration includes however
long the caller holds the connection, so it is not a connection-set-up measurement — it is a
connection-lifetime measurement that happens to be dominated by set-up today. Name it
`boundary.session`, or close the span after the handshake and let the lifetime be implied by
`boundary.call`."*

**The interview question.** *"Here is a trace. One span, four hundred milliseconds, and the service it
calls reports a two-millisecond response time. Where is the time, and what span would you add to find
out? Now tell me how you would have known to look without the downstream service's number."*
