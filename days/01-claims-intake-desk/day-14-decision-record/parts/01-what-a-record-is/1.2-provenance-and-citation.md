## One-line answer

Provenance answers *who said this and what it cost*, the citations answer *which rule a handler would
have read*, and neither is part of the derivation — which is exactly why they are in separate sections
of the record from the facts.

## The idea

There are two questions an auditor asks after *does it follow*, and they are not the same question.

**Which model said this?** A claim decided against a model pinned to one ID is a different claim from
one decided against a model that was hot-swapped last Tuesday — day 1 built a whole registry to refuse
`-latest` aliases for exactly this reason. The answer has to be on the record, because the agent that
produced the reading will have been rebuilt a hundred times by the time anybody asks.

**Which rule does the department say this is?** Not the code — the handbook. A handler shown a
decision will not read `assess`; they will look up the section and see whether the desk did what the
department says. Day 12 made those sections citable and the citations checkable, and this is what that
was for.

Both are necessary and **neither is evidence for the verdict.** That distinction is why the record has
three sections rather than one flat object, and it is worth being strict about, because the natural
instinct is to flatten everything into one dictionary and let the reader sort it out.

Consider what flattening costs. A record where `model` sits beside `in_force` invites the reading that
the model's identity was an input to the decision. It was not: `replay` ignores every field in
`Provenance`, and part 3.1 asserts that by changing the model name and the call counts and confirming
the verdict is unmoved. The separation in the file is a claim about what mattered, made structurally
rather than in prose.

And there is a fourth thing in the record that is none of the above: the letter. A policyholder
received it, so it belongs with the claim. It is prose, so it cannot be evidence. Its `description`
in the schema says exactly that, and it is the only field in the file that needs to.

## The mechanism

The two sections, from `claims_desk/record.py`, printed whole in part 1.1:

```python
class Provenance(BaseModel):
    """Who produced the reading, at what cost, and against which rules.

    Not part of the derivation — `replay()` ignores every field here — and an auditor needs it
    anyway, because "which model said this" is the first question after "does it follow".
    """

    model_config = ConfigDict(extra="forbid")

    rules_version: str = Field(default=RULES_VERSION, description="The version of `assess`.")
    model: str = Field(description="The model name, exactly as the agent was pinned to it.")
    calls: dict[str, int] = Field(description="Model calls by agent, from the run's own budget.")
    handbook: list[str] = Field(
        default_factory=list,
        description="Citations for the rules a handler would read. Each one must resolve.",
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- **The docstring's second sentence is the design**: `replay()` ignores every field here. Somebody
  tempted to move a field between `Facts` and `Provenance` needs to know that the boundary decides
  whether the field is evidence.
- `rules_version: str = Field(default=RULES_VERSION, ...)` — **defaulted, so every record gets it
  without a caller remembering.** A field an auditor needs on every record and nobody would think to
  pass is a field with a default.
- `model: str` — **the name, exactly as pinned.** Day 1's registry refuses `-latest` so this string
  names a model rather than a role, and day 7's `scripted/` prefix means a record produced by the
  stand-in says so in the one field anybody will read.
- `calls: dict[str, int]` — **day 9's breakdown, by agent.** Not a total: a claim that cost one
  classification and two drafts is a different claim from one that cost three classifications, and
  the second one is a claim somebody should look at.
- `handbook: list[str]` **with `default_factory=list`** — a mutable default needs a factory, which is
  the same lesson day 11's `ContextVar` taught from the other direction.
- **The description says "Each one must resolve."** A requirement stated in the schema and enforced in
  part 3.1, which is the right division: the schema cannot check it, so it says it and a test does it.

And the mapping from a verdict to the sections a handler would read:

```python
#: The handbook sections a handler would read for each reason, so a record carries the rule in the
#: department's own words as well as in code. Written by hand from `data/handbook.md`, because a
#: mapping guessed by a retriever is a citation nobody has checked.
CITES: dict[str, tuple[str, ...]] = {
    Reason.MISSING_DATE_OF_LOSS: ("handbook.md#HB-05",),
    Reason.MISSING_CONTACT: ("handbook.md#HB-05",),
    Reason.UNKNOWN_POLICY: ("handbook.md#HB-06",),
    Reason.POLICY_NOT_IN_FORCE: ("handbook.md#HB-06",),
    Reason.PERIL_NOT_COVERED: ("handbook.md#HB-01",),
    Reason.MISSING_PHOTO_REFERENCE: ("handbook.md#HB-02", "handbook.md#HB-01"),
    Reason.INJURY_REPORTED: ("handbook.md#HB-03", "handbook.md#HB-02"),
    Reason.ABOVE_FAST_TRACK_LIMIT: ("handbook.md#HB-04", "handbook.md#HB-01"),
}
```

**Line by line:**

- **"Written by hand ... because a mapping guessed by a retriever is a citation nobody has checked."**
  Day 12 built a retriever that would happily produce a section for each reason, and its own
  `recall@3` is 0.9. A record is not the place for a one-in-ten chance.
- **Keyed by `Reason` members, not by strings.** A reason added to the enum without an entry here is
  caught by a test rather than by producing a record with no citation.
- `MISSING_DATE_OF_LOSS` **and** `MISSING_CONTACT` **both cite HB-05** — the deficiency-letter rule.
  Neither has a section of its own, because the handbook does not have one: what a handler needs to
  know about either is how to write the letter.
- `MISSING_PHOTO_REFERENCE` **cites HB-02 then HB-01**, in that order — the photograph rule first,
  because that is the rule that fired, and the fast-track rule second, because HB-02's exceptions only
  make sense beside it.
- `INJURY_REPORTED` **cites HB-03 then HB-02** — the injury rule, and then the photograph rule, which
  is the section that says a photograph is *never* required where an injury is reported. A handler
  reading only HB-03 might still ask for one.
- `ABOVE_FAST_TRACK_LIMIT` **cites HB-04**, which is the section that says a handler must never quote
  the limit — the rule day 10's house style enforces in code, cited here from the record that names
  the amount as the reason.
- **Order is meaningful and tuples preserve it.** A set would have been the natural type for "which
  sections" and would have thrown away which one fired.

**Line by line:**

- **`decision` is nested inside the claim file rather than replacing it.** Day 6's three fields are
  still at the top level, so every report and test written since day 6 keeps working. The record is
  additive.
- **`claim_id` appears twice, and that is deliberate** — once on the file and once inside the record.
  A record extracted from its file and emailed to somebody still says which claim it is about, and
  part 3.1 shows the boundary refusing a record whose id does not match the claim it is filed under.
- `"estimate": 4800` **against** `"fast_track_limit": 2500` — **the two numbers the rule compared,
  both present.** An auditor can do the comparison themselves, which is the whole design.
- `"perils": ["escape-of-water", "fire", "storm"]` **and** `"loss_type": "escape-of-water"` — the
  peril check passed, visibly. The record shows not just the rule that fired but the rules that did
  not, because the facts they read are all there.
- `"model": "scripted/claims-classifier"` — **this claim was decided by a test double and the record
  says so.** Day 7 built that prefix so a transcript could not be mistaken for evidence; here it
  stops a whole claim being mistaken for a real one.
- `"calls": {"classifier": 1}` — **one model call for this claim.** The letter took a draft too, and
  it is not here: `calls` is the budget breakdown at the moment the verdict was reached, and the
  letter was written afterwards. That is a real gap and part 2.1's `TODO(me)` is to close it.
- `"rules_version": "2026-09-11.a"` — the rule set. If `assess` changes tomorrow, a replay of this
  record against the new rules may produce a different verdict, and this field is what says the two
  are not comparable.
- `"handbook": ["handbook.md#HB-04", "handbook.md#HB-01"]` — **two citations, in the order a handler
  should read them.** HB-04 is the limit rule; HB-01 is what the fast-track is for.
- **The letter is stored twice** — at the top level from day 9 and inside the record. Redundant, and
  the redundancy is not free: two copies can disagree. Worth a review comment, and the reason it is
  here is that the record is meant to be extractable on its own.
- **The keys are alphabetical inside `decision` and not inside the claim file.** `model_dump` sorts
  nothing; pydantic preserves field order, and `json.dumps` of the outer dict preserves insertion
  order — so what looks like a sorting convention is two different orderings meeting. Worth knowing
  before writing a test that compares serialised records byte for byte.

**Line by line:**

- **Three empty values, and every one of them is a fact.** `loss_type` is empty because nothing read
  the description; `model` is empty because no model was asked; `calls` is empty because nothing was
  spent.
- **This is day 7's ordering showing up in the record.** The desk decides everything it can from
  fields before asking a model, so four of twelve claims have a record that says *no model was
  involved* — which is the strongest possible answer to "was this decided by AI".
- `loss_type: ''` **replays correctly** because `assess` checks the date of loss first and returns
  before it ever looks at the peril. The record is honest about the order of the rules by being
  honest about what was never needed.
- **An empty string rather than a null.** A null would invite "we do not know what the peril was"; an
  empty string in a field whose sibling says no model was called reads as "not applicable". Neither
  is perfect and this is the one a test can assert.
- `cites: ['handbook.md#HB-05']` — **a claim decided with no model still cites the handbook.** The
  rule is the department's either way.

## When it breaks

Use the retriever to fill in the citations — the obvious labour saving, and day 12 built exactly the
tool for it:

```python
    handbook=[section["citation"] for section in recall.cited(reason, 2)["sections"]],
```

Nothing fails. Every citation resolves, because part 12's `resolves` is satisfied by any real
section. `defensible` is unaffected, because `replay` ignores provenance. Every test in this project
stays green.

And one claim in ten cites the wrong rule, which is day 12's own measured number: `recall@3` is 0.9,
and `recall@1` is 0.7.

Work out what that means on the worst case day 12 actually found. A claim refused for
`above_fast_track_limit` would be asked as the question *"above_fast_track_limit"* — and day 12 part
2.2 measured the closest thing to that phrasing reaching **HB-01** rather than HB-04, with the wrong
answer scoring `0.406` against the right one's `0.194`. So the record for a claim refused on the limit
would cite *What the fast-track is for* and not *The fast-track limit*, confidently, with a citation
that resolves.

A handler follows it, reads a section about eligibility, and cannot see why the claim was refused.
Worse, an auditor follows it and reads the record as saying the desk applied the eligibility rule,
which it did not.

**A citation that resolves is not a citation that is right**, and day 12 part 2.1 said so in those
words. The difference between the two is a test, and no test in this project can check the mapping
from a reason to a section — which is precisely why `CITES` is eight lines of hand-written dictionary
rather than two lines of retrieval.

The smallest fix is not using the retriever. The general rule: **retrieval is for questions, not for
records.** A question tolerates being approximately right because a person reads the answer and
judges it. A record is read as an assertion, by somebody who was not there, possibly years later, and
there is nobody left to notice that the citation is nearly relevant.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same mapping, with the handbook section's own version or
last-changed date recorded beside the citation — because `handbook.md#HB-04` cited today and followed
in two years leads to whatever HB-04 says then, which may not be what it said when the claim was
decided. Day 12 part 1.1 named that as the thing a senior would insist on early, and this is the
record that needs it.

**What degrades at scale.** Eight reasons and eight entries is a mapping one person holds in their
head. A real intake desk has several dozen reason codes across a handbook of several hundred
sections, and the hand-written mapping becomes the thing nobody maintains — at which point the
temptation to generate it returns with much better arguments. What survives is the discipline rather
than the method: whatever produces the mapping, **a test asserts every reason has at least one
citation and every citation resolves**, and somebody reads the list once a year.

**The review comment.** *"`calls` is the budget breakdown at the moment of the verdict, so every
deficiency record under-reports by however many drafts the letter took. Either build the record after
the letter, or rename the field to say what it counts."*

**The interview question.** *"Your decision record cites an internal policy document. The document is
edited. What have you actually recorded, and what would you need to change so that the citation still
means something?"*
