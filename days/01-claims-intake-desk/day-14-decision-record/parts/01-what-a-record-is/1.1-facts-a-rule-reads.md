## One-line answer

A decision record holds exactly what the rules read — not a trace, not a log, not a summary — so that
the outcome can be re-derived from the record alone, and a field in it that no rule consults is a
field somebody will read as evidence.

## The idea

An auditor does not ask what you decided. The claim file has said that since day 6.

They ask **why**, and the question has a specific shape that is easy to miss. It is not *tell me your
reasoning*. It is: **show me that this outcome follows from your own rules and the facts you had.**
Two things, both of which have to be in front of them, and a conclusion they can reach themselves
without taking anybody's word.

Most of what a system naturally keeps fails that test.

**A trace fails.** Day 11's plugin writes a line per model call, day 10's workflow writes a line per
stage, and together they say exactly what happened in order. An auditor cannot re-derive a verdict
from a sequence of events; they can only confirm that the sequence occurred.

**A log fails, and for a reason worth keeping.** Day 11 established that a log records *how much* and
never *what* — the number of characters, not the letter. That is correct for a log, because a log is
retained under different rules and read by more people. It also means a log can never be the evidence.

**A model's own explanation fails hardest.** Ask the classifier why and you get fluent prose that may
or may not describe what the rules did. Day 9 made the same argument about delegation: *why did this
claim go to correspondence* should be answerable by reading a rule, not by asking a model what it
decided.

What passes is narrow and slightly dull: **the inputs to the rule, the rule that fired, and the
outcome** — written so that a function can take the record and nothing else and produce the verdict
again.

That gives the record's contents a hard test, and it is subtractive rather than additive. The record
holds what `assess` reads. Not what would be nice to have, not what was easy to include: the eleven
facts the eight rules consult. Every extra field is a thing an auditor will read as having mattered.

## The mechanism

The whole file, at `claims_desk/record.py`:

```python
"""The decision record: everything needed to re-derive one verdict, and nothing else.

An auditor does not ask what the desk decided. The claim file has said that since day 6. They ask
**why**, and the answer has to satisfy a test no log line can: *show me that this outcome follows
from your own rules and the facts you had at the time.*

That is a stronger requirement than it sounds, and it rules out most of what a system naturally
keeps. A trace is not a decision record — it says what happened, in order, and an auditor cannot
re-derive anything from it. A log is not one either: day 11 established that a log records how much
and never what, which is right for a log and useless here.

A decision record is the **inputs** to a rule, the rule that fired, and the outcome — written so
that `replay()` below can take the record alone, with no store, no boundary and no model, and
produce the same verdict. A record that cannot be replayed is a claim this desk cannot defend.

**No prose anywhere in it.** The letter is stored beside the record because a policyholder received
it; nothing in the record is a summary of anything, because a summary is a thing that has to be
believed rather than checked.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from datetime import date
from typing import Any

from pydantic import BaseModel, ConfigDict, Field

from claims_desk.domain import Cover, Notification, Outcome, Peril, Reason, assess

#: The version of the rules that decided a claim. It changes when `assess` changes — the order of
#: the checks, the checks themselves, or `PHOTO_REQUIRED_FOR`. A record carries it because a rule
#: set is part of the answer to "why", and a replay against different rules is not a replay.
RULES_VERSION = "2026-09-11.a"


class Facts(BaseModel):
    """Everything `assess` reads, flattened, with nothing that it does not read.

    The discipline is the whole point: a field in here that no rule consults is a field somebody
    added because it seemed relevant, and it will be read as evidence by whoever audits this.
    """

    model_config = ConfigDict(extra="forbid")

    policy_number: str = Field(description="The policy the notification quoted.")
    date_of_loss: date | None = Field(description="Absent is a fact; the first rule turns on it.")
    contact_ref: str | None = Field(description="Absent is a fact; the second rule turns on it.")
    photo_ref: str | None = Field(description="Absent is a fact for some perils and not others.")
    estimate: int = Field(description="What the policyholder reported, in whole pounds.")
    policy_found: bool = Field(description="Whether the boundary knew the policy at all.")
    in_force: bool = Field(description="Whether it was on risk on the date of loss.")
    perils: list[str] = Field(description="What the policy covered, as the boundary returned it.")
    fast_track_limit: int = Field(description="This policy's own limit, from its schedule.")
    loss_type: str = Field(description="What the classifier read, already validated by its schema.")
    injury_reported: bool = Field(description="What the classifier read about an injury.")


class Provenance(BaseModel):
    """Who produced the reading, at what cost, and against which rules.

    Not part of the derivation — `replay()` ignores every field here — and an auditor needs it
    anyway, because "which model said this" is the first question after "does it follow".
    """

    model_config = ConfigDict(extra="forbid")

    rules_version: str = Field(default=RULES_VERSION, description="The version of `assess`.")
    model: str = Field(description="The model name, exactly as the agent was pinned to it.")
    calls: dict[str, int] = Field(description="Model calls by agent, from the run's own budget.")
    handbook: list[str] = Field(
        default_factory=list,
        description="Citations for the rules a handler would read. Each one must resolve.",
    )


class DecisionRecord(BaseModel):
    """One claim, decided, in a form somebody can check without trusting anybody."""

    model_config = ConfigDict(extra="forbid")

    claim_id: str
    outcome: str
    reason: str | None
    facts: Facts
    provenance: Provenance
    letter: str | None = Field(
        default=None,
        description="What went out, stored because a policyholder received it — not as evidence.",
    )


def facts_from(
    notification: Notification, cover: Cover | None, loss_type: str, injury_reported: bool
) -> Facts:
    """Flatten the four arguments `assess` takes into the record's own shape.

    `cover is None` becomes `policy_found=False` with empty cover fields rather than a missing
    section, because an auditor reading a record should not have to distinguish "we did not look"
    from "we looked and found nothing". The record says we looked.
    """
    return Facts(
        policy_number=notification.policy_number,
        date_of_loss=notification.date_of_loss,
        contact_ref=notification.contact_ref,
        photo_ref=notification.photo_ref,
        estimate=notification.estimate,
        policy_found=cover is not None,
        in_force=cover.in_force if cover else False,
        perils=sorted(cover.perils) if cover else [],
        fast_track_limit=cover.fast_track_limit if cover else 0,
        loss_type=loss_type,
        injury_reported=injury_reported,
    )


def replay(record: DecisionRecord) -> tuple[str, str | None]:
    """Re-derive the outcome from the record alone. No store, no boundary, no model.

    This is the function an auditor is really asking about. It rebuilds the two objects `assess`
    wants out of the record's own facts and runs the same rules — so a record that replays to a
    different verdict is a record whose claim this desk cannot defend, and part 2.1 is what that
    looks like.
    """
    facts = record.facts
    notification = Notification(
        id=record.claim_id,
        policy_number=facts.policy_number,
        description="",
        estimate=facts.estimate,
        date_of_loss=facts.date_of_loss,
        photo_ref=facts.photo_ref,
        contact_ref=facts.contact_ref,
    )
    cover = (
        Cover(
            in_force=facts.in_force,
            perils=frozenset(facts.perils),
            fast_track_limit=facts.fast_track_limit,
        )
        if facts.policy_found
        else None
    )
    outcome, reason = assess(notification, cover, facts.loss_type, facts.injury_reported)
    return outcome.value, (reason.value if reason else None)


def defensible(record: DecisionRecord) -> bool:
    """Does the record replay to the verdict it claims? The one question that matters."""
    return replay(record) == (record.outcome, record.reason)


def which_rule(record: DecisionRecord) -> str:
    """The name of the rule that decided this claim, derived rather than recorded.

    Derived on purpose. A recorded rule name is a second thing that can be wrong, and it would be
    wrong in the direction nobody checks — an auditor comparing the stated rule against the outcome
    would be comparing two fields written by the same code. This reads the reason, which `replay`
    has already proved follows from the facts.
    """
    if record.outcome == Outcome.FAST_TRACK:
        return "no rule fired: every check passed"
    return f"{record.reason}"


def as_stored(record: DecisionRecord) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """The record as it goes into the claim file: JSON-ready, with dates as strings."""
    return record.model_dump(mode="json")


#: The handbook sections a handler would read for each reason, so a record carries the rule in the
#: department's own words as well as in code. Written by hand from `data/handbook.md`, because a
#: mapping guessed by a retriever is a citation nobody has checked.
CITES: dict[str, tuple[str, ...]] = {
    Reason.MISSING_DATE_OF_LOSS: ("handbook.md#HB-05",),
    Reason.MISSING_CONTACT: ("handbook.md#HB-05",),
    Reason.UNKNOWN_POLICY: ("handbook.md#HB-06",),
    Reason.POLICY_NOT_IN_FORCE: ("handbook.md#HB-06",),
    Reason.PERIL_NOT_COVERED: ("handbook.md#HB-01",),
    Reason.MISSING_PHOTO_REFERENCE: ("handbook.md#HB-02", "handbook.md#HB-01"),
    Reason.INJURY_REPORTED: ("handbook.md#HB-03", "handbook.md#HB-02"),
    Reason.ABOVE_FAST_TRACK_LIMIT: ("handbook.md#HB-04", "handbook.md#HB-01"),
}

#: What a fast-track cites. One section, because there is one rule that says what the fast-track is
#: for and a claim that passed every check is a claim that rule allows.
FAST_TRACK_CITES: tuple[str, ...] = ("handbook.md#HB-01",)


def cites(reason: str | None) -> list[str]:
    """The handbook citations for one verdict. Every one of them resolves; part 3.1 asserts it."""
    if reason is None:
        return list(FAST_TRACK_CITES)
    return list(CITES.get(reason, ()))


def peril_is_known(record: DecisionRecord) -> bool:
    """Was the recorded loss type one this desk recognises when the record was written?

    Day 13's schema refuses an unknown peril at the moment of answering. This asks the same question
    of a record read back later, when the enum may have moved — which is the audit question rather
    than the runtime one.
    """
    return record.facts.loss_type in {peril.value for peril in Peril}
```

**Line by line:**

- **The docstring rules three things out by name** — a trace, a log, a model's explanation — before
  saying what a record is. Somebody reaching for this file probably already has two of the three and
  needs to know why they are not enough.
- **"No prose anywhere in it."** The one sentence that decides the shape. A summary is a thing that
  has to be believed; every field below is a thing that can be checked.
- `RULES_VERSION = "2026-09-11.a"` — **a string, bumped by hand, and the comment says exactly what
  changes it**: the order of the checks, the checks themselves, or `PHOTO_REQUIRED_FOR`. A replay
  against different rules is not a replay, so the record has to say which rules.
- **It is not derived from a git hash on purpose.** A hash changes when a comment changes, so a
  record would claim a new rule set on every commit and an auditor would learn to ignore the field.
- `class Facts(BaseModel)` with **`extra="forbid"`** — day 13's setting, here for a second reason:
  it stops a well-meaning field being added. The schema is the discipline.
- `date_of_loss: date | None = Field(description="Absent is a fact; the first rule turns on it.")` —
  **the description says why `None` is meaningful.** Three of these eleven fields are optional and in
  every case the absence is what a rule tests. A reader who did not know that would treat a null as
  missing data.
- `policy_found: bool` — **a separate field from `in_force`**, because "we could not find the policy"
  and "we found it and it was not on risk" are two different letters under day 2's rules and two
  different conversations with an auditor.
- `perils: list[str]` **rather than `set`** — JSON has no sets, and the record's job is to be
  serialisable. `facts_from` sorts it, so two records with the same cover are byte-identical.
- `loss_type: str` **and not `Peril`** — deliberately looser than day 13's schema. At write time the
  peril is validated; a record read back in a year must still **load** when the enum has moved, so
  that somebody can see what it says. `peril_is_known` asks the question separately.
- `class Provenance` — **four fields, none of which the replay reads.** That separation is the point:
  provenance answers *who said this*, facts answer *does it follow*, and mixing them invites an
  auditor to treat the model name as part of the justification.
- `calls: dict[str, int]` — **day 9's budget breakdown, stored.** What a claim cost, by agent, which
  is the number an insurer actually wants when it asks what automated handling is saving.
- `handbook: list[str]` — the citations day 12 made checkable. A record that cites the rule in the
  department's own words is a record a handler can argue with; part 1.2 is about that.
- `class DecisionRecord` with `letter` **last and optional**, and its description doing the most
  important job in the file: *stored because a policyholder received it — not as evidence.* The
  letter is the only prose in the record and the description says it is not part of the derivation.
- `def facts_from(...)` — **the flattening lives here, not in the desk.** One function turns the four
  arguments `assess` takes into the record's eleven fields, so the record and the rules cannot drift.
- `policy_found=cover is not None` **with empty cover fields rather than an absent section** — and
  the docstring gives the reason: an auditor should not have to distinguish *we did not look* from
  *we looked and found nothing*. The record says we looked.
- `def replay(record)` — **the function the whole day is about.** It takes a record and returns a
  verdict, and its signature is the promise: one argument, no services.
- `Notification(..., description="")` **inside the replay** — the description is not a fact any rule
  reads, so the record does not hold it, so the replay passes an empty string. That is not a gap; it
  is the subtraction working, and part 3.1 asserts the policyholder's words are nowhere in the record.
- `assess(notification, cover, facts.loss_type, facts.injury_reported)` — **the same function the
  desk called.** Not a copy of the rules, not a reimplementation: if this were a second copy, a
  replay would be proving that two copies agree rather than that the verdict follows.
- `def defensible(record)` — **one comparison, and it is the audit.** `replay(record) == (outcome,
  reason)`. Everything else in this file exists so that this line can be written.
- `def which_rule(record)` — **derived, and the docstring argues for that at length.** A stored rule
  name would be a second field written by the same code that wrote the outcome, so comparing them
  would prove nothing.
- `return f"{record.reason}"` — the reason *is* the rule's name, which is day 2's design paying off:
  eight rules, eight reason codes, one name each.
- `def as_stored(record)` — `model_dump(mode="json")`, so `date` becomes `"2026-06-08"` and the
  record can be written to a claim file by the boundary without anything knowing about dates.
- `CITES` **keyed by `Reason` members**, so a reason added to the enum without a handbook section is
  caught by part 3.1's `test_every_reason_the_desk_can_give_has_citations` rather than producing a
  verdict nobody can look up.
- **`MISSING_PHOTO_REFERENCE` cites two sections and `UNKNOWN_POLICY` cites one.** The number is not
  uniform because the handbook is not: the photograph rule has exceptions that live in HB-01, and a
  record should cite what a handler would actually have to read.
- `def cites(reason)` with `reason is None` **meaning fast-track** — the same convention day 6 chose
  for `Verdict.reason`, reused rather than re-decided.
- `def peril_is_known(record)` — **the only function here that is about reading a record later.**
  Day 13 refuses an unknown peril at write time; this asks whether a stored one is still known, which
  is a question only an audit asks.

## When it breaks

Add a field to `Facts` that no rule reads. The obvious candidate, and somebody will ask for it:
the policyholder's own description, *so the auditor can see what we were working from.*

Nothing breaks. The record gets bigger, `replay` ignores the field, `defensible` still returns
`True`, and part 3.1's `test_the_facts_are_exactly_what_assess_reads` goes red with a one-item
difference.

So the failure is not mechanical, and it is worth being precise about what it actually is.

**An auditor reads every field in a record as having mattered.** That is not naivety; it is the only
reasonable way to read a document called a decision record. Put the description in `Facts` and you
have asserted, in the strongest form available to you, that the words the policyholder used were an
input to the decision. They were not. No rule reads them. The classifier read them and produced
`loss_type`, and *that* is the input — which is why it is the field in the record.

The version of this that causes real damage is the one that looks most reasonable: include the
description, and now a claim refused for `peril_not_covered` has the policyholder's own account of a
covered peril sitting beside it in the record. Every word of it true, none of it an input, and an
auditor entitled to ask why it was ignored.

The smallest fix is deleting the field. The rule to carry: **a decision record is defined by
subtraction.** Start from what the rule reads and refuse everything else, including things that are
true, relevant and helpful — because a record is read as a claim about what was decisive.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same three models, with the record written **before** the
decision is acted on rather than after — this desk builds it alongside the verdict, and a system
where the letter goes out first and the record is assembled later is a system whose records are
reconstructions. What they would add is a signature or a hash over the stored record, because
`defensible` proves the facts produce the verdict and proves nothing about whether the facts were
edited afterwards. Part 2.1 is exactly that limit.

**What degrades at scale.** A record is roughly 700 bytes of JSON beside a claim file of about 200, so
recording quadruples the store's size — trivial at twelve claims, and at a morning's several hundred
notifications it is still a few hundred kilobytes a day. What does not scale is `RULES_VERSION` as a
hand-bumped string: the first time somebody changes the order of two checks in `assess` and forgets
it, every record from that day claims rules it was not decided under, and nothing detects it. The
thing to build before that happens is a test that fails when `assess` changes and the version does
not.

**The review comment.** *"`Facts` has `perils` as the whole covered list. That is what `assess` reads,
so it is right — but it means every record carries the policy's full cover, and cover is commercially
sensitive. Check with whoever owns the policy data whether a record that names what was *not* covered
is something we are allowed to keep per claim."*

**The interview question.** *"Design the record your system would hand an auditor who asks why one
decision went the way it did. Tell me what you would refuse to put in it, and why."*
