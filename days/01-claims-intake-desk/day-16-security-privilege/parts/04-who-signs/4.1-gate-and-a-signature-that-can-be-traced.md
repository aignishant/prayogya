## One-line answer

Above an estimate of 2500 the desk decides and then **holds** — the verdict is reached, nothing is
written to the claim file, and the claim is counted as pending until a person approves it; the approval
itself is a record naming who signed, which claim, and the figure they were shown, because a signature
with no statement of what was in front of the signer is not an audit trail.

## The idea

There is a signature book in every claims office, and the interesting thing about it is not the
signatures.

A handler settles claims up to their authority limit on their own. Above it they prepare the file and
take it to somebody who can authorise more. What that person does is not re-do the work — they read what
the handler concluded, look at the figure, and sign. The file then carries two things: the decision, and
the fact that a named person accepted it at a stated amount.

Two details of that arrangement are easy to get wrong when you build it in software.

The first is **what the limit restricts.** It does not restrict the handler's judgement. The handler
still assesses the claim, still reaches a conclusion, still writes it down. The limit restricts what can
be *acted on* without a second person. Software versions of this routinely get it backwards — above the
threshold, stop early, do not assess, escalate a bare claim — which throws away the work and hands a
human a blank file, so the human does the assessment, so the automation did nothing on exactly the
claims where help is worth most.

The second is **what gets recorded.** "Approved by handler-7" is almost useless six months later. The
question an auditor asks is not whether somebody signed but what they signed *for*, because the defence
against a rubber-stamped approval is being able to show the approver was looking at a figure of 4800
rather than the 800 somebody later claims was on screen. A signature on a blank page is the thing to
avoid, and the cure is to record the fact that triggered the gate alongside the name.

Now the threshold itself, because choosing the number is where this becomes real rather than decorative.

Every fast-track limit in this desk's policy data is 2500 or under. A fast-track is, by definition, a
claim at or under its policy's limit. So **a threshold above 2500 can never hold a fast-track** — and the
figure chosen is 2500 exactly, which is the fast-track ceiling: the line above which this desk is no
longer settling small claims. In this queue one claim is above it — FNOL-4481, a mains pipe under a
driveway, estimate 4800, which the rules call a deficiency precisely because it is too large to
fast-track. The one claim the rules say is too big to settle quickly is the one claim the gate holds.
That coherence is not a coincidence; it is what choosing the threshold from the data rather than from a
round number buys.

It is worth being blunt about the alternative, because a gate at 10000 would have been the comfortable
choice. No test would have had to change, nothing in the queue would behave differently, the whole thing
would have read as working — and the gate would never once have fired against this project's own data.
A control that cannot be observed to work is indistinguishable from one that does not.

**Line by line:**

- `APPROVAL_ABOVE = 2_500` — **the underscore is for the reader.** At four digits it barely matters; the
  habit is what stops `25000` from being mistaken for `2500` in a review of a file full of numbers.
- **The comment justifies the figure from the data**, which is the part that makes the constant
  reviewable. `APPROVAL_ABOVE = 2_500` on its own invites "why 2500?" and gets no answer; the comment
  says it is the fast-track ceiling and states the consequence.
- `return outcome != "fast-track" and estimate > APPROVAL_ABOVE` — **`>` and not `>=`.** A claim of
  exactly 2500 is at the ceiling, settles as a fast-track, and is not held. Drawing the line at *above
  the limit* rather than *at the limit* matches how an authority limit reads on paper, and the tests pin
  2500 as the boundary case precisely because `>=` is the plausible typo.
- **The `outcome` check is redundant and stays.** As the docstring says, no fast-track can exceed the
  threshold anyway. It is there because the next person to move `APPROVAL_ABOVE` downwards — and
  somebody will, the day a regulator asks for more oversight — would otherwise silently start holding
  fast-tracks. A guard that costs nothing and survives a future edit is worth its line.
- `needs_approval` **takes two plain values and returns a boolean.** No notification, no verdict, no
  session. That is what makes the parametrised test cheap enough to cover all four corners, and it means
  the function has no way to reach out and hold anything itself.
- `approval_note` **returns a dictionary and logs.** Both, deliberately: the log line is the operational
  signal and the returned dictionary is what day 14's record carries, and the two have different
  lifetimes — a log is rotated, a record is kept with the claim.
- `estimate` **is in the note, and the docstring says why.** It is the fact that triggered the gate, so
  it is the fact the approver must have been looking at.
- `approver: str` — **an identifier, not a name.** `handler-7` is what an access system can resolve and
  what an auditor can trace; a human name in a claim file is a second copy of personal data with a
  longer retention period than anybody intended.

**Line by line:**

- **The gate is after `decide` and before the write.** That is the first idea from above, expressed as
  placement: the assessment has already happened, so the held claim arrives at a human with a verdict, a
  reason and a decision record attached, not as a blank file.
- `if verdict.decided and ...` — **a pending verdict is never held.** A claim the desk could not decide is
  already waiting for a person, and holding it for approval as well would report it twice and imply a
  decision exists.
- **It is also before the letter.** A held claim costs one model call rather than four, because the
  letter-writing loop never runs. That is worth knowing and it is not the reason for the placement: the
  reason is that a deficiency letter is an action with an outcome attached, and sending one says *your
  claim is deficient* before anybody has agreed to say it.
- `counts[verdict.outcome] -= 1` **then** `counts[PENDING] += 1` — **the correction is ugly and it is
  honest.** The count was incremented the line before, and the neat version moves the increment below
  the gate. This form was kept because it makes the held claim's journey visible in the code: it was
  counted as a deficiency, and then it was not one, because nobody has agreed to it yet. The total is
  unchanged, which the tests assert.
- `continue` — **no write, no letter, nothing.** The claim file for a held claim does not exist, which is
  the property part 5.1's test asserts by name. A record saying `pending` would be a decision about a
  claim nobody has approved.
- **The comment says where the approval will go**, and is honest that it has not gone there yet. Day 14
  built the record; wiring `approval_note` into it is a `TODO(me)` in the checklist rather than a claim

The log line still appears. The event still fires. Anybody watching the logs sees `awaiting_approval` on
FNOL-4481 and concludes the gate is working — and the claim is in the claim file, decided, with a letter
sent to the policyholder saying the claim is deficient.

**A gate that logs and does not stop anything is theatre**, and it is a particularly bad kind because it
produces exactly the evidence that makes people believe the control exists. The failure is not in
`needs_approval`, which is correct and well tested in isolation; it is in where it was called. The
smallest fix is to move the check back above the write. The general rule: **a control's position in the
sequence is part of the control.** Test it where it acts, not where it is defined — which is why the test
that catches this asserts on the contents of a directory rather than on a return value.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** A held claim that goes somewhere. Here the gate logs and the claim is
counted pending, and a person finds it by reading logs, which is not a queue. The real shape is a hold
written to a durable work list with the verdict and the record attached, an identity on the approval
rather than a string, and `record_decision` accepting the approval as a required argument for anything
above the threshold — so the boundary refuses to write a held decision that arrives without one, and
the gate stops depending on the desk remembering to ask.

**What degrades at scale.** One claim in twelve here. An insurer's household book has a long tail: a
threshold at the fast-track ceiling catches everything from a kitchen ceiling down to a subsidence
claim, which at eight thousand notifications a day is several hundred holds — more than a supervisory
team can read. That is where the threshold stops being one number and becomes a matrix by peril and by
product, because 4800 on a burst pipe and 4800 on a liability claim do not want the same level of
attention, and a single figure buys oversight on the wrong claims.

**The review comment.** *"`counts[verdict.outcome] -= 1` immediately after incrementing it is a smell
even though the total is right. Move the increment below the gate and the arithmetic disappears. And
`approval_note` is never called by anything in this desk — either wire it into the record now or mark it
`TODO` in the module, because an audit function with no call site is a function that will not work when
somebody finally needs it."*

**The interview question.** *"You have a human approval step above a threshold. Tell me what happens to
the work your system already did on that item — and then tell me what your approval record contains,
and why each field is in it."*
