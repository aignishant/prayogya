## One-line answer

`fetch_policy` returns nine fields and the policy record on disk has ten — the policyholder's reference
and the address of the risk never cross the boundary, because no rule in this desk reads them; and the
failure this part exists for is that widening it to return the whole record **breaks nothing, logs
nothing, and writes nothing to disk**, which is why it needs a test rather than vigilance.

## The idea

The counter at the front of a claims office has a hatch, and what goes through the hatch is a
deliberate subset of what is in the back.

A handler asks the policy department whether cover was in force on the eighth of June and what the
fast-track limit is. What comes back is an answer to that. What does not come back is the
policyholder's address, because the question was about cover. Nobody describes this as data
minimisation; it is just that you ask for what you need and the person answering has no reason to read
out the rest.

Software does the opposite by default, and the reason is worth naming because it is not laziness.
Returning the whole record is *easier to maintain*. Write `return dataclasses.asdict(policy)` and the
next field somebody adds to the policy model is available to callers without anybody touching this
function. A projection — nine named keys — has to be edited when the model changes. So the wide version
wins on the merits, on every axis except the one that matters here.

What it costs is this. The moment `fetch_policy` returns the record, the policyholder's address is in
the desk's process. Not used by anything. Not written anywhere. Just present — in the process that runs
models, holds the provider key, assembles prompts and writes logs. And the set of things that can go
wrong with data present in a process is not the set of things that can go wrong with data that was
never sent: a prompt assembled from a dictionary by a later change, a log line that dumps a tool result
for debugging, a traceback captured by an error reporter, a heap dump.

The address is worth a moment too, because "PII" is a category that invites hand-waving. This is home
insurance. The `risk_address` is **the address of the property being insured**, which for a home policy
is where the policyholder lives. Paired with `holder_ref` and a note saying the house has fire damage
and nobody is living in it, it is a more sensitive artefact than most of what people label PII — and
the set of people who can see it should be the set who need to, which is the policy department and the
adjuster, and not an automated triage desk.

So the rule this desk follows is: **the boundary answers questions, it does not hand over records.**
Nine fields, each one read by a rule. And the tests that hold it have to do something slightly unusual,
which is to assert that the fields *are* in the store as well as *not* in the answer — because a test
that only checks the answer passes perfectly when the data was never there, and then somebody adds the
address to the fixture and the test is still green and means nothing.

## The mechanism

No new code. The projection has been there since day 5, and the whole of this part is about pinning it
and then trying to break it. What crosses the boundary, printed against what is on disk:

```
on disk        : ['excess', 'expiry', 'fast_track_limit', 'holder_ref', 'inception', 'number', 'perils', 'product', 'risk_address', 'status']
across the wire: ['excess', 'expiry', 'fast_track_limit', 'found', 'inception', 'number', 'perils', 'product', 'status']
withheld       : ['holder_ref', 'risk_address']
```

**Line by line:**

- **Ten fields on disk, nine across the wire, and the nine are not a subset of the ten** — `found` is in
  the answer and not in the record, because day 5 decided a policy number nobody has heard of is an
  answer and not an error. So this is a projection with a field added, not a filter.
- `withheld: ['holder_ref', 'risk_address']` — **two fields, and the desk has never needed either.**
  Every rule in `domain.py` reads a date, a limit, a peril list, an excess or a status.
- **`number` does cross**, which is worth noticing before somebody calls it an inconsistency. The desk
  asked about a policy number; echoing it back is how a caller matches an answer to a question, and it
  is data the desk already had.

And the tests. Three of them, and the third is the one that makes the other two mean something:

```bash
uv run pytest tests/test_security.py -q -p no:warnings -k "policy_record or personal or does_hold"
```

```
...                                                                      [100%]
3 passed, 22 deselected in 6.33s
```

**Line by line:**

- The first fetches one policy and asserts neither personal field is in the answer, **and** that the
  answer still has `found`, `perils` and `fast_track_limit` — the positive half, so that a
  `fetch_policy` which returned `{}` could not pass.
- The second does the same for **every policy in the fixture** rather than the convenient one, which is
  the test that would catch a projection applied in one code path and not another.
- The third asserts every record on disk **does** hold both fields, and its docstring says why:
  *otherwise the test above passes because the data is not there, which proves nothing.* That is the
  most easily skipped test of the three and the one that keeps the pair honest.
- `PERSONAL = ("holder_ref", "risk_address")` **is a module constant**, so the three tests cannot drift
  apart, and adding a third sensitive field to the fixture means adding one name in one place.

## When it breaks

Here is the change, and it is one line plus an import. Somebody is tired of editing the projection
every time the policy model gains a field:

```diff
 from __future__ import annotations
 
+import dataclasses
+
@@
     policy = store.policy(number)
     log("boundary.fetch_policy", number=number, found=policy is not None)
     if policy is None:
         return {"found": False, "number": number}
-    return {
-        "found": True,
-        "number": policy.number,
-        "product": policy.product,
-        "status": policy.status,
-        "inception": policy.inception.isoformat(),
-        "expiry": policy.expiry.isoformat(),
-        "excess": policy.excess,
-        "fast_track_limit": policy.fast_track_limit,
-        "perils": sorted(policy.perils),
-    }
+    return {"found": True, **dataclasses.asdict(policy)}
```

Eleven lines become one. It is shorter, it is more maintainable by any ordinary measure, and a reviewer
looking at a diff that *deletes* code is predisposed to approve it.

Now run everything this project had before today:

```
uv run pytest -q -p no:warnings --ignore=tests/test_security.py

200 passed in 20.81s
```

**Two hundred tests, all green.** Read that again, because it is the entire point of the part. Fifteen
days of careful work — the domain rules, the boundary, the tools, the cast, the workflow, the hooks, the
schema, the decision record, the reliability suite — and not one of them notices that the desk is now
receiving a policyholder's home address.

Then run the whole queue and go looking for the evidence:

- Nothing in `data/claims/` contains `holder_ref`, `risk_address`, or the address string. Day 14's
  record stores the facts a rule read, and no rule read either field.
- Nothing in the logs carries it. `boundary.fetch_policy` logs the number and whether it was found,
  never the answer.
- No behaviour changes anywhere. Four fast-tracked, seven recorded, one held — identical.

The reason is `Cover.from_boundary`, which pulls exactly three values out of whatever dictionary it is
given and drops the rest. So the address arrives in the desk's process, is ignored by the next function
that touches it, and is garbage collected. **There is nothing to see.** That is not a happy accident, it
is the normal case: a data leak into a process is invisible precisely because nothing uses the data.

Today's tests are the only thing that disagrees:

```
    def test_the_boundary_returns_cover_and_not_the_policy_record() -> None:
        """The record holds a person's address. The rules need a limit and a peril list."""
        answer = asyncio.run(boundary.fetch_policy("SYN-POL-1001"))
        for field in PERSONAL:
>           assert field not in answer
E           AssertionError: assert 'holder_ref' not in {'found': True, 'number': 'SYN-POL-1001', 'holder_ref': 'H-2201', 'risk_address': '12 Kiln Row, Barrowfield', ...}

tests\test_security.py:167: AssertionError
```

There it is in the failure message: `'risk_address': '12 Kiln Row, Barrowfield'`, in a dictionary the
desk is holding.

The smallest fix is to put the projection back. The general rule is the one this failure is built to
teach, and it is the reason today exists at all: **a control whose violation produces no symptom must be
held by a test, because there is nothing else that can hold it.** A bug announces itself. A widened
boundary does not announce anything — it waits for the later change that reads the dictionary it should
never have had.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The projection as a declared output type rather than a dictionary
literal — a `Cover` model on the server side whose fields are the nine, with the tool's return
annotated as that type, so adding a field to the policy model cannot widen the answer and a reviewer
sees a schema change rather than a one-line refactor. The maintenance complaint that motivated the bad
diff is real, and this is the answer to it: make the narrow thing cheap to maintain instead of relying
on people not taking the shortcut.

**What degrades at scale.** Nine fields is reviewable. A real policy record in home insurance carries
somewhere around two hundred — cover sections, endorsements, previous claims, the broker, bank details
for the premium, occupancy and construction detail — and a projection with two hundred named keys is
not something anybody reads. At that size the shape that survives is an explicit allowlist of field
*paths* held as data beside the threat model, with a test that the projection's output keys are a subset
of it, because the review question stops being "is this field needed" and becomes "who added a field
and when".

**The review comment.** *"`asdict` here widens the tool's answer to whatever the policy model happens to
hold, now and in future. The eleven-line version is not worse than the one-line version; it is the
interface. If editing it on every model change is the objection, make the return type explicit so the
compiler does the editing."*

**The interview question.** *"Your tool returns a customer record to an agent. Tell me how you would
find out whether any field in it is unnecessary — and then tell me what test you would write, given
that removing an unnecessary field breaks nothing and adding one back breaks nothing either."*
