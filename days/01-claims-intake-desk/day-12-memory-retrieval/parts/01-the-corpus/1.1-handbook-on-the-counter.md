## One-line answer

The thing a desk needs to look up is not last week's caller — it is the department's own handbook,
a fixed corpus that is the same for every claim and every policyholder, and writing it down with an
identifier per rule is what makes a citation something a reader can follow.

## The idea

There is a folder on the counter that nobody files anything into.

It is the handbook: which perils need a photograph, what the fast-track is for, how many sentences a
deficiency letter may be. A handler reaches for it several times a morning, and what they are doing
when they reach for it is the opposite of remembering. They are *looking something up* — because the
answer is the same for every claim, it was written by the people who answer for it, and a handler
who relied on memory would be relying on the wrong copy.

That distinction is the whole of today, and it is worth stating before any code:

- **Memory** is what a system keeps from one piece of work into the next. It is per-person, it grows,
  and it is the thing this desk's own handbook forbids at HB-07.
- **Retrieval** is looking something up in a body of text that does not change per claim. It is
  shared, it is versioned, and nothing in it belongs to anybody.

Almost everything a claims desk is tempted to "remember" turns out to belong in the second category
— and the small remainder that does not is usually something it should not be keeping at all.

So the first thing this day builds is not a retriever. It is the corpus, because **a retrieval
system is mostly its corpus**, and the two decisions that matter are made when the corpus is
written: what a section is, and what a section is called.

A section is one rule, written by one team, complete. It is not six hundred characters. If a rule
runs to a paragraph, the section is a paragraph; if it needs three, the section is three. The reason
is that a top-k answer hands somebody a section and says *this is what the handbook says* — and half
a rule can say the opposite of the rule.

And a section has an **identifier that never moves**. `HB-02` is the photograph rule. It is not "the
second heading", not "line 34", not "the section about photographs". An answer that cites `HB-02` is
checkable in ten years by anybody holding the file, and that is the only kind of citation worth
printing.

## The mechanism

The whole file, at `data/handbook.md`:

```markdown
# Claims Intake Handbook

Synthetic. Every rule, figure and name in this file was invented for this project and describes no
real insurer. It is the department's own guidance, written for the people on the counter, and the
desk looks things up in it rather than remembering them.

## HB-01 · What the fast-track is for

Fast-track exists to settle small, clear household claims without an adjuster visit. A claim may be
fast-tracked only when the policy was on risk on the date of loss, the peril is one the policy
covers, and the reported estimate is at or below the policy's fast-track limit. Nothing else about
the claim makes it eligible, and no handler may fast-track a claim on the grounds that the
policyholder is in a hurry.

## HB-02 · When a photograph is required

A photograph of the damage is required for every escape-of-water and accidental-damage claim before
the claim can be fast-tracked. It is not required for windscreen, which the repairer inspects, nor
for theft, where the police reference stands in its place. A photograph is never required from a
policyholder who has reported an injury.

## HB-03 · Injuries stop the fast-track

Any claim where a person is reported hurt leaves the fast-track immediately and goes to an adjuster,
whatever the estimate is and whatever the peril. The handler does not ask for further documents and
does not request a photograph. The letter says only that an adjuster will call.

## HB-04 · The fast-track limit

Each policy carries its own fast-track limit and the schedule is the only place it is recorded. Most
household policies are written at two thousand five hundred pounds; some are lower. A handler must
never quote the limit to a policyholder, because the figure belongs to the schedule and a quoted
figure becomes a statement the company has made.

## HB-05 · Deficiency letters

A deficiency letter names one thing and asks for it. It does not list alternatives, does not promise
a decision or a timescale, and never contains an amount of money. Four sentences is the house
maximum. The wording for each reason is set by the correspondence team and handlers use it as
written.

## HB-06 · Policies not on risk

Where the policy quoted was not on risk on the date of loss, the claim is a deficiency and the
letter asks the policyholder which policy was in force on that date. The handler does not search for
another policy on the policyholder's behalf and does not tell them the quoted policy has lapsed.

## HB-07 · What the desk must not keep

Nothing a policyholder says on the telephone is kept beyond the claim it was said about. Contact
details, the description of the loss and any note a handler makes are held against the claim
reference and are not carried into another claim, another policyholder's file, or any list used to
answer later questions.

## HB-08 · Escalation

A handler who is unsure escalates rather than deciding. Escalation is not a failure and is not
counted against a handler. A claim escalated on the day it is notified is cheaper than a claim
fast-tracked wrongly and reopened three weeks later.
```

**Line by line:**

- **"Synthetic" is the first word after the title**, before any rule. Everything in this project is
  invented, and a document that looks like an insurer's internal guidance is the one file most
  likely to be lifted out of context and quoted at somebody.
- `## HB-01 · What the fast-track is for` — **an identifier and a title on the same line.** The
  identifier is the citation and the title is what a human reads in a list of results; neither can
  do the other's job.
- **`HB-01`, not `1`.** A bare number is a position and positions move. A prefixed identifier is a
  name, and a name survives somebody inserting a rule in the middle.
- **Eight sections, one rule each.** HB-02 is the whole photograph rule including its two exceptions;
  splitting the exceptions into their own chunk would let a retriever hand back "a photograph is
  required for every escape-of-water claim" without the sentence that says *never from a
  policyholder who has reported an injury*.
- **HB-01 states three conditions and then closes them**: *"Nothing else about the claim makes it
  eligible."* A corpus written for retrieval says what is **not** true, because a section that only
  lists positives reads as a partial list to anybody who finds it alone.
- **HB-03 and HB-02 deliberately overlap.** The photograph rule says a photograph is never required
  where an injury is reported; the injury rule says the handler does not request one. Two sections
  that agree are how a corpus survives being read one section at a time.
- **HB-04 never states the limit as a figure the desk may use** — it says the schedule is the only
  place it is recorded, and that a handler must not quote it. That is day 10's house style, written
  down where it came from.
- **HB-05 is the letter rules this project has been enforcing in code since day 9.** Putting them in
  the handbook is not duplication: the code is where they are enforced, and this is where they are
  *from*. When the correspondence team changes its mind, this file changes first.
- **HB-07 is the rule this whole day answers to.** *"Nothing a policyholder says on the telephone is
  kept beyond the claim it was said about"* — including "any list used to answer later questions",
  which is precisely what a memory service is.
- **No policyholder, claim reference, telephone number or address appears anywhere in the file.**
  That is what makes one index shareable across every claim, and part 4.1 asserts it rather than
  trusting it.
- **HB-08 gives a reason and a number-shaped comparison** — *cheaper than a claim fast-tracked
  wrongly and reopened three weeks later* — because a rule that says "escalate when unsure" without
  saying why is a rule handlers quietly stop following.

Read the file the way the code will:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk import recall
for chunk in recall.chunks():
    print('%-6s %-34s %4d chars  %s' % (chunk.id, chunk.title, len(chunk.text), chunk.citation))
"
```

```
HB-01  What the fast-track is for          412 chars  handbook.md#HB-01
HB-02  When a photograph is required       336 chars  handbook.md#HB-02
HB-03  Injuries stop the fast-track        280 chars  handbook.md#HB-03
HB-04  The fast-track limit                345 chars  handbook.md#HB-04
HB-05  Deficiency letters                  297 chars  handbook.md#HB-05
HB-06  Policies not on risk                292 chars  handbook.md#HB-06
HB-07  What the desk must not keep         312 chars  handbook.md#HB-07
HB-08  Escalation                          242 chars  handbook.md#HB-08
```

**Line by line:**

- **Eight chunks, and their sizes range from 242 to 412 characters** — a spread of roughly seventy
  per cent. A fixed-size chunker would have produced eight equal blocks and cut four of these rules
  in half; the variation is the document's own, and it is the evidence that nothing was cut.
- `handbook.md#HB-02` — **the citation, built from the identifier**, and it looks like a URL fragment
  because that is what it will become the day this file is served rather than read.
- **The title comes out of the heading, not out of a separate table.** One line in the source is the
  identifier, the title and the boundary between two chunks, which means those three things cannot
  drift apart.

## When it breaks

Delete the ` · ` from one heading — the separator, not the identifier — and re-run the same command:

```
HB-01  What the fast-track is for          790 chars  handbook.md#HB-01
HB-03  Injuries stop the fast-track        280 chars  handbook.md#HB-03
HB-04  The fast-track limit                345 chars  handbook.md#HB-04
HB-05  Deficiency letters                  297 chars  handbook.md#HB-05
HB-06  Policies not on risk                292 chars  handbook.md#HB-06
HB-07  What the desk must not keep         312 chars  handbook.md#HB-07
HB-08  Escalation                          242 chars  handbook.md#HB-08
```

Verbatim, from this project. `HB-02` is gone and `HB-01` is 790 characters where it was 412: the
photograph rule has been swallowed by the section **above** it, heading and all. Nothing failed. The
file is still valid markdown, the code still runs, and seven chunks come back where there were
eight.

The direction is the part people get wrong. A section is the text from its own heading to the *next*
heading, so a heading that stops being recognised does not lose its own section — it dissolves into
its predecessor. The rule that disappears is not the one you edited.

What happens next is worth sitting with. A handler asks whether a windscreen claim needs a
photograph. The retriever returns `HB-01`, and the citation reads *handbook.md#HB-01 · What the
fast-track is for* — a real section, a citation that resolves, and the wrong rule. **The citation
still works.** It points at text that exists, a reader can follow it, and it is misleading.

The smallest fix is the separator. The lesson is that **a corpus has a schema even when it is
prose**, and the chunker is the thing that enforces it. Part 4.1's
`test_the_handbook_splits_at_its_own_headings` asserts all eight identifiers in order, which is what
turns a silent absorption into a red test.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same file, with the identifiers issued from somewhere that
refuses to reuse one — a rule that is deleted leaves its identifier retired rather than free, so a
citation written last year cannot silently start pointing at a new rule. What they would insist on
early is **a last-changed date per section**, because the first question anybody asks about a cited
rule is whether it was the rule at the time of the claim.

**What degrades at scale.** Eight sections is a corpus you can read in full, and every number in
this day was computed over all eight. A real intake handbook is several hundred sections across a
dozen documents, at which point two things break that are invisible here: sections start
contradicting each other, and the retriever's job changes from *find the rule* to *find the rule
that is still in force*. Neither is a retrieval problem, and both get handed to the retriever.

**The review comment.** *"This chunker splits on `##`. The claims handbook has `###` sub-sections
under three of its rules and they are about to be swallowed into their parents. Decide what a chunk
is before the corpus grows, not after."*

**The interview question.** *"You are given a policy manual to make searchable. Before you write any
retrieval code, what do you need to decide about the document, and who decides it?"*
