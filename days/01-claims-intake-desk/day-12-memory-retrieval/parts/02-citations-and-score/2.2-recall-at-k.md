## One-line answer

`recall@k` is the share of questions whose right section is somewhere in the top `k`, measured
against a set of questions somebody wrote by hand — and this desk's is 0.7 at k=1, 0.9 at k=3, with
one question it gets wrong at every k and a named reason why.

## The idea

Every retrieval demonstration works. That is what makes them demonstrations.

Somebody types a question, the right section comes back, everyone agrees it is impressive, and the
thing ships. Three months later a handler follows a citation to the wrong rule and the conversation
starts with *"but it worked when we tried it"* — which is true, and is the problem: **three questions
chosen by the person who built the retriever are not a measurement.**

A measurement needs a set of questions written down before the answers are known, by somebody who
read the corpus rather than the code, with the right answer recorded next to each one. Then the score
is arithmetic and it can go down.

`recall@k` is the simplest such score and the right one to start with: for each question, is the
expected section anywhere in the top `k` results? The share that are is the recall. Nothing about
ordering within the k, nothing about how many wrong sections came with it — just *did it get there*.

The `k` is the interesting parameter, because it is a statement about the reader. `k=1` is a system
that answers. `k=3` is a system that hands somebody three rules and expects them to pick. Those are
different products, and the recall of each says what each one costs.

And the thing that makes this a real measurement rather than a number: **the misses are reported by
name.** A recall of 0.9 tells you one question in ten fails. Which one it is decides whether that
matters, and a score without the misses beside it is a number nobody can act on.

## The mechanism

The whole file, at `data/handbook_questions.json`:

```json
[
  {
    "asked": "does an escape of water claim need a photograph before fast-track",
    "expect": "HB-02",
    "why": "the photograph rule names escape-of-water directly"
  },
  {
    "asked": "somebody was hurt on this one, can it still be fast-tracked",
    "expect": "HB-03",
    "why": "an injury leaves the fast-track whatever the estimate"
  },
  {
    "asked": "am I allowed to tell the caller what the fast-track limit is",
    "expect": "HB-04",
    "why": "the limit belongs to the schedule and is never quoted"
  },
  {

  },
  {

  },
  {

    "why": "nothing said on the telephone is kept beyond the claim it was said about"
  },
  {
    "asked": "I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it or pass it on",
    "expect": "HB-08",
    "why": "escalation is not a failure and is cheaper than a wrong fast-track"
  },
  {
    "asked": "does a windscreen claim need a photograph",
    "expect": "HB-02",
    "why": "the same rule, from its exceptions rather than its subject"
  },
  {
    "asked": "what makes a household claim eligible for the fast-track at all",
    "expect": "HB-01",
    "why": "three conditions, and nothing else about the claim"
  },
  {
    "asked": "may a deficiency letter mention how much we will pay",
    "expect": "HB-05",
    "why": "a letter never contains an amount of money"
  }
]
```

**Line by line:**

- **A data file, not a Python list.** The people who should be adding questions are the people who
  answer the telephone, and a JSON file is something they can be shown. A list in a module is not.
- `"asked"` **in the words a handler would use**, not in the corpus's words. *"Can I keep a note of
  what this caller told me for next time"* shares almost nothing with HB-07's phrasing, which is the
  point: a question written out of the section is a question the retriever cannot fail.
- `"expect"` **is one identifier, not a list.** Several sections may be relevant; exactly one is the
  rule that answers the question. Allowing a set of acceptable answers is how an evalset stops being
  able to fail.
- `"why"` **on every entry, and nothing reads it.** It is there for the person who finds a miss in
  six months and has to decide whether the expectation or the retriever is wrong. Without it, the
  temptation on a red score is always to change the expectation.
- **Ten questions, eight sections, two sections asked about twice.** HB-02 and HB-05 each get two
  questions, phrased from different angles — one from the rule's subject, one from its exceptions.
  That is deliberate: a section that is only ever asked about one way is a section whose recall is
  about one phrasing.
- **`"does a windscreen claim need a photograph"` asks HB-02 from its exception.** The section's
  subject is escape-of-water and accidental-damage; windscreen appears only in the sentence saying a
  photograph is *not* required. A retriever that only matches subjects fails this and it should.
- **The two HB-05 questions are the same rule from opposite ends** — how long may a letter be, and
  may it mention money. One of them works and one does not, which is measured below rather than
  guessed.

And the measurement, from `claims_desk/recall.py`, printed whole in part 1.2:

```python
def recall_at_k(k: int = 3, index: Index | None = None) -> dict[str, object]:
    """The share of questions whose expected section is somewhere in the top `k`.

    **A number that can go down.** It is reported with the misses named, because a score on its own
    tells you that something changed and never which question stopped working.
    """
    import json

    asked = json.loads(QUESTIONS.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))
    engine = index or Index()
    misses = []
    for case in asked:
        got = [chunk.id for chunk, _ in engine.top(case["asked"], k)]
        if case["expect"] not in got:
            misses.append({"asked": case["asked"], "expect": case["expect"], "got": got})
    hits = len(asked) - len(misses)
    return {
        "k": k,
        "asked": len(asked),
        "hit": hits,
        "recall": round(hits / len(asked), 3),
        "misses": misses,
    }
```

**Line by line:**

- `if case["expect"] not in got:` — **membership in the top k, not position.** That is what makes it
  recall rather than precision or mean reciprocal rank, and it is the right first measure: get the
  rule in front of the reader, then argue about where in the list it sits.
- `misses.append({"asked": ..., "expect": ..., "got": got})` — **the question, what it should have
  reached, and what it did reach.** All three, because a miss with only the first two is a bug report
  with the evidence missing.
- `"k": k` **in the returned dictionary** — the score is meaningless without it, and a score that
  travels into a log or a report without its k will eventually be compared against one taken at a
  different k.
- `"asked": len(asked)` **beside the recall** — `0.9` over ten questions and `0.9` over four hundred
  are different facts, and the second number is what says which.
- `round(hits / len(asked), 3)` — **three decimals**, so ten questions produce `0.7`, `0.8`, `0.9`
  and not `0.7000000000000001`.
- `engine = index or Index()` — **the index is passed in for a reason.** Measuring at three values of
  k rebuilds the index three times otherwise, and the score would be the same but the run would read
  the handbook off disk three times over.

Measure it at three values of `k`:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk import recall
index = recall.Index()
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    measured = recall.recall_at_k(k, index)
    print('recall@%d = %.3f  (%d of %d)' % (k, measured['recall'], measured['hit'], measured['asked']))
    for miss in measured['misses']:
        print('    miss  %-50s expected %s  got %s' % (miss['asked'][:50], miss['expect'], miss['got']))
"
```

```
recall@1 = 0.700  (7 of 10)
    miss  am I allowed to tell the caller what the fast-trac expected HB-04  got ['HB-01']
    miss  I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it expected HB-08  got ['HB-07']
    miss  may a deficiency letter mention how much we will p expected HB-05  got ['HB-01']
recall@2 = 0.800  (8 of 10)
    miss  I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it expected HB-08  got ['HB-07', 'HB-01']
    miss  may a deficiency letter mention how much we will p expected HB-05  got ['HB-01', 'HB-03']
recall@3 = 0.900  (9 of 10)
    miss  I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it expected HB-08  got ['HB-07', 'HB-01']
```

**Line by line:**

- **0.700, 0.800, 0.900.** Recall rises with k by construction — a bigger net catches more — and the
  shape of the rise is the useful part: two of the three k=1 misses are recovered by k=3 and one is
  not.
- **Three misses at k=1, and they are not the same kind of failure.** That matters more than the
  score: one is a phrase problem, one is a paraphrase problem, and one is a genuine confusion between
  two related rules.
- `expected HB-04 got ['HB-01']` — **the phrase problem**, and part 1.2 already showed it: "fast
  track limit" is three separate terms, HB-01 is full of "fast-track", and it wins by more than
  double. Recovered at k=2.
- `expected HB-05 got ['HB-01']` then `['HB-01', 'HB-03']` — **the paraphrase problem.** The question
  says *"mention how much we will pay"*; HB-05 says *"never contains an amount of money"*. They share
  no rare word at all. Recovered at k=3, and only by luck.
- `expected HB-08 got ['HB-07']` — **the same miss at every k, and it is the interesting one.** The
  question is about escalating; the retriever reaches the rule about what the desk must not keep.
- **`got` gets longer as k grows and the expected id never appears.** HB-08 is not fourth or fifth;
  it is nowhere near. Increasing k would not fix this one, which is exactly what a per-k miss list is
  for.

## When it breaks

The escalation miss is the failure this part exists for, because it is the one that does not respond
to any of the obvious fixes.

The question: *"I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it or pass it on."* The right answer
is HB-08, which says a handler who is unsure escalates rather than deciding. What comes back is
HB-07, *What the desk must not keep*.

Ask the index what the two sections and the question actually have in common:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk import recall
index = recall.Index()
question = 'I am not sure about this claim, should I decide it or pass it on'
asked = set(recall.words(question))
print('question terms:', sorted(asked))
for section in ('HB-07', 'HB-08'):
    chunk = next(c for c in index.chunks if c.id == section)
    shared = sorted(asked & set(recall.words(chunk.title + ' ' + chunk.text)))
    print(section, 'shared:', shared, ' idf:', {t: round(index.idf.get(t, 0), 2) for t in shared})
"
```

```
question terms: ['about', 'am', 'decide', 'i', 'pass', 'should', 'sure', 'this']
HB-07 shared: ['about']  idf: {'about': 1.39}
HB-08 shared: []  idf: {}
```

Verbatim, from this project, and it is worse than a near miss.

**The question and the right section share no terms at all.** Not one. HB-08 says "unsure" where the
question says "sure", "escalates" where the question says "pass it on", and "deciding" where the
question says "decide" — and with no stemming, every one of those pairs is two unrelated words. The
cosine between the question and the correct answer is exactly zero.

**HB-07 wins on the word "about".** One term, shared by accident, with an idf of 1.39 — high, because
"about" happens to appear in only two of the eight sections, which makes this retriever treat it as
informative. It is not informative. It is a preposition that survived a short stop list.

So the top result is not a close second-best. It is the only section with a non-zero score, and the
score comes from a word that means nothing. A retriever cannot rank what it cannot see, and it
cannot see HB-08 at all.

Three fixes suggest themselves and it is worth knowing what each is really doing:

- **Add "unsure" and "escalate" to the question.** This is editing the exam. The retriever has not
  improved; the question has been rewritten in the corpus's words, which is the thing part 2.2's
  evalset was written to avoid.
- **Add a stemmer** so `sure`/`unsure` and `decide`/`decided` collapse. A real improvement, and it
  would also make `photograph`/`photographs` one term — and it is a change whose effect on the other
  nine questions has to be measured, not assumed.
- **Use an embedding model**, where "unsure" and "escalate" and "pass it on" land near each other
  because a model has read enough English to know they belong together. That is the fix that
  actually addresses this failure, and it is the one this project cannot run.

This project takes none of them, and says so: `recall@3` is 0.9, the floor in part 4.1 is 0.9, and
**the one question it fails is named in a test** so that nobody discovers it by accident. The
`TODO(me)` in the hub is the stemmer, with the instruction to measure all ten questions before and
after rather than the one.

The rule worth carrying: **a retriever that cannot see past the words is a retriever whose failures
are all the same failure.** Every miss on this page is a vocabulary mismatch. That is a comfort — it
is one problem, not three — and it is also the ceiling: no amount of tuning a bag of words teaches it
that "pass it on" means "escalate".

And one more thing this failure shows that the score alone does not. **A zero-scoring right answer is
invisible to `k`.** Part 2.1's filter drops sections that score zero, so HB-08 could not be returned
at k=8 either; the miss is not that the net was too small, it is that the fish was never in the
water.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same measurement, run in the test suite exactly as this one
is, plus a second score that notices ordering — `MRR`, or recall@1 tracked separately — because
recall@3 stays flat while the top result quietly gets worse. What they would insist on is that the
evalset grows from **real questions**: every time a handler follows a citation to the wrong rule,
that question goes in the file with the right answer beside it, and the score is allowed to fall
when it does.

**What degrades at scale.** Ten questions over eight sections gives a recall that moves in steps of
0.1, so a single new question changes the headline number more than most real improvements do. At a
few hundred questions the score becomes stable enough to trend, and the thing that degrades instead
is the *evalset itself*: expectations written against a handbook that has since been reworded, and
nobody notices because the questions still pass. Part 4.1's
`test_the_evalset_only_expects_sections_that_exist` catches the crudest version of that and not the
subtle one.

**The review comment.** *"recall@3 went from 0.9 to 1.0 in this change and the diff is three edits to
`handbook_questions.json`. Either the retriever improved or the exam got easier — say which, and if
it is the second one, revert it and open a ticket for the stemmer."*

**The interview question.** *"Your retrieval scores 0.9 on recall@3. What do you need to know before
you have any idea whether that is good?"*
