## One-line answer

Disable the policy in-force check and `./run eval` goes red on ten claims — one on its verdict and
nine on their route — which is the failure this project's brief named on day 0 as the one the eval
must catch, and the trajectory marks are what catch it on the nine claims whose verdicts were still
right.

## The idea

The reason a claims department keeps a file audit is not the score. It is the week the score drops.

Here is the shape of that week. A handler on the desk decides, reasonably, that the cover query is
slowing everything down: the policy department takes a day to answer, most policies are in force
anyway, and the queue is long. So they stop sending it. For a fortnight, nothing happens — the
claims they decide are the same claims, decided the same way, and eleven of twelve come out
identical. Then the audit lands, and it fails ten of their twelve files: nine for a missing query,
and one for a claim they fast-tracked on a policy that had lapsed six months before the loss.

Notice what caught which. The lapsed policy was caught by the verdict column — the decision was
wrong, and any audit would have seen it. The other nine were caught by the *policy checked?* column,
on files whose decisions were correct. An audit with only a verdict column would have failed one file
in twelve and the handler would have argued the case: one mistake, ninety-two per cent, a bad week.
An audit with the trajectory column fails ten, and there is no argument, because the audit is not
saying the decisions were wrong. It is saying the process that is supposed to produce right decisions
was not followed, and the one wrong decision is what that process exists to prevent.

That is the gate: a check that goes red when the system gets worse, **in a way that names what got
worse**, and that stops the change going any further. This project's brief has said since day 0
what this desk's gate must catch: *the eval goes red the moment the policy in-force check is
disabled*. Today that sentence gets tested, by doing the thing it describes.

There is one more thing to notice before the transcript, because it is the reason this part is the
day's deliberate failure rather than a check-box. Disabling the in-force check is not an exotic
edit. It is four lines, it makes the desk faster — one fewer boundary launch per claim, which day
17 measured at 1.7 seconds each — and it leaves 273 of the desk's 280 tests green. Somebody would do
it. The question is what tells them.

## The mechanism

The edit, in `claims_desk/desk.py`, in `decide`. Not a marked diff to keep — a marked diff to make,
watch, and revert:

```diff
     policy = await boundary.fetch_policy(notification.policy_number)
-    in_force = False
-    if policy.get("found") and notification.date_of_loss is not None:
-        answer = await boundary.check_in_force(
-            notification.policy_number, notification.date_of_loss
-        )
-        in_force = bool(answer.get("in_force"))
+    in_force = bool(policy.get("found"))
     cover = Cover.from_boundary(policy, in_force) if policy.get("found") else None
```

**Line by line:**

- `in_force = bool(policy.get("found"))` — **a policy that exists is assumed to be on risk.** This
  is the plausible version of the mistake, not the absurd one: it keeps the unknown-policy rule
  working, it keeps every field rule working, and it is wrong only for a policy that exists and was
  not on risk on the date — which in this queue is exactly one claim, FNOL-4474, on a policy that
  lapsed the May before the loss.
- **One boundary call per claim goes away.** Day 17 measured a boundary launch at 1.7 seconds on
  this machine, so the edit takes roughly ten of those out of a sixty-eight-second run. It is a
  real speed-up, which is why somebody makes it.

## When it breaks

The gate itself can be broken, and the way it breaks is quieter than any of the above. Make the audit
lenient about the route — say, because somebody added a tool and did not want to rewrite the
expected route for twelve cases:

```diff
-    result.marks.append(Mark("tools", expected_tools(expected, held), called))
+    result.marks.append(Mark("tools", set(expected_tools(expected, held)) <= set(called), True))
```

That version asks only whether every expected tool appears somewhere in the route. It still fails the
in-force edit — `check_in_force` is absent, not reordered — so nobody notices the change. What it no
longer fails is a desk that records the decision *before* drafting the letter, or fetches the policy
twice, or calls `check_in_force` after the verdict rather than before it. The first of those writes a
claim file with `letter: null` and then drafts a letter nobody stores. The gate stays green.

There is no error text for this failure, and that is the point of it. **A gate that has been
loosened does not go red. It goes quiet.** The smallest fix is a test on the gate — part 4.1's
inverted case, which changes the answer key and asserts the audit objects — and the discipline that
every loosening of a mark is a pull request whose description says which failure it can no longer
see.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The gate in the pipeline, not on a developer's machine: `./run
eval` runs on every change, its exit status blocks the merge, and its report is posted where the
reviewer reads it. Two thresholds rather than one — the absolute floor, twelve of twelve here, and
a **no-regression** rule against the previous release's report, so a change that turns a nine-mark
claim into an eight-mark one is red even if the claim still passes. And a rule that the answer key
is changed in a separate commit from the code, reviewed by someone who did not write either.

**What degrades at scale.** This gate runs the whole desk, which day 17 timed at sixty-eight seconds
for twelve claims — a minute a run. A three-hundred-case evalset on the same desk is twenty-eight
minutes, and a gate that takes half an hour is a gate people run "later". The answer is the split
this day already made: `./run check` in seconds on every save, the audit in the pipeline on every
push, and the full evalset — with a real provider, at a real cost per claim — nightly, with its
report reviewed in the morning. Three gates at three speeds, and the slow one is the only one that
can see a lapsed policy.

**The review comment.** *"The in-force edit is caught by ten claims, which is the right answer for a
gate and the wrong answer for a report. Ten red lines that all say the same thing bury the one that
says something different. Group the route failures: `check_in_force missing on 9 claims`, then
FNOL-4474's seven marks on their own. The reader should see the wrong verdict first."*

**The interview question.** *"Your evaluation suite is green and a change has just made the system
worse. Give me two concrete ways that happens, and for each one tell me what kind of check would
have caught it."*
