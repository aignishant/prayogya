## One-line answer

A retry is three attempts with day 1's waits between them, the provider's own `Retry-After` beating
that schedule whenever it is given, a cap past which "wait" is really a refusal, and a budget guard
that will not spend the last call in the allowance finding out there is none left.

## The idea

Day 1 built `util/backoff.py` before this project had anything to retry.

That looked odd at the time and the reason is in its own docstring: *a claims desk that invents an
in-force date because the provider was busy has not degraded gracefully; it has made something up
about a policy.* The module was written early because the temptation it defends against arrives on
the first bad afternoon, and by then nobody is writing careful code.

Today is that afternoon, and the whole day is one sentence enforced: **when the desk cannot get an
answer, it says so.**

Four things have to be decided to make that work, and each of them is a place where a reasonable
person does the wrong thing.

**How many times.** Three, including the first. The second attempt recovers a blip, the third
recovers a restart, and a fourth mostly recovers nothing while delaying an escalation somebody could
have been acting on. More attempts feels safer and is mostly a longer wait before the same answer.

**How long between.** Day 1's schedule — 1 second, then 2 — unless the provider says otherwise, in
which case **it wins.** A `Retry-After` is not advice; it is the other side telling you what it can
take, and arithmetic that overrides it is arithmetic making the outage worse.

**Except when what it says is really a refusal.** A provider asking for an hour is not asking you to
wait; it is telling you to stop. Past a cap — thirty seconds here — the honest response is to
escalate, because a queue that sleeps for an hour holding a claim is a queue nobody can reason
about.

**And whether you can afford it.** This is the one day 1 could not have known about. A retry is
another model call, day 11's plugin charges for it, and day 9's ceiling is per claim. A retry the
budget cannot pay for must not be attempted at all — otherwise the desk spends its last call
discovering it has none, and the claim after this one pays for the discovery.

## The mechanism

The whole file, at `claims_desk/reliability.py`:

```python
"""What this desk does when the provider is not there, and what it refuses to do instead.

Day 1 built `util/backoff.py` on the first day this project had any code, before there was anything
to retry. The reason it was written that early is the sentence in its own docstring: *a claims desk
that invents an in-force date because the provider was busy has not degraded gracefully; it has made
something up about a policy.* Everything here is that sentence enforced.

Four mechanisms, in the order a failure meets them:

- **Retry**, but only what is safe to retry, and only for as long as the caller's own budget allows.
- **`Retry-After`**, when the other side says how long — a provider's instruction beats our
  arithmetic every time, which is why day 1's `Transient` carries the interval.
- **The idempotency key**, so a retry of a *write* cannot produce two of anything. A read is free to
  repeat; recording a decision is not.
- **Escalation**, when the attempts run out — a pending claim with a reason, never a fabricated
  answer.

There is no fallback model in here and that is deliberate. A cheaper model asked the same question
gives a different answer, and a claim decided by whichever model happened to be up is a claim whose
record cannot say what decided it. Day 14 made that a property this desk keeps; part 2.1 is the
argument in full.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import hashlib
import json
from collections.abc import Awaitable, Callable
from typing import Any

from claims_desk.util.backoff import Transient, delays
from claims_desk.util.logging import log

#: Attempts, including the first. Three because the second is what recovers a blip and the third is
#: what recovers a restart; a fourth mostly recovers nothing and delays an escalation somebody could
#: have been acting on. The waits are day 1's 1, 2, 4 — the first two of them.
ATTEMPTS = 3

#: What the desk waits between attempts when the provider does not say. Day 1's schedule, taken
#: rather than reinvented, because a project with two backoff curves has one nobody has measured.
WAITS = tuple(delays(ATTEMPTS, base=1.0))

#: The longest this desk will honour a provider's own `Retry-After`. A provider asking for an hour
#: is telling you to stop, not to wait — and a queue that sleeps for an hour holding a claim is a
#: queue nobody can reason about. Past this, escalate.
LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT = 30.0


class Unavailable(RuntimeError):
    """The attempts ran out. The caller must escalate, never substitute."""

    def __init__(self, what: str, attempts: int, last: Exception) -> None:
        super().__init__(f"{what} failed after {attempts} attempts: {last}")
        self.what = what
        self.attempts = attempts
        self.last = last


def wait_for(attempt: int, failure: Transient) -> float:
    """How long to wait before attempt `attempt + 1`, honouring what the provider asked for.

    The provider's own interval wins, capped: `LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT` is where "wait" stops being
    an instruction and starts being a refusal. When it says nothing, day 1's schedule applies.
    """
    if failure.retry_after is not None:
        return min(failure.retry_after, LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT)
    return WAITS[min(attempt - 1, len(WAITS) - 1)]


async def with_retry[T](
    what: str,
    call: Callable[[], Awaitable[T]],
    *,
    attempts: int = ATTEMPTS,
    sleep: Callable[[float], Awaitable[None]] | None = None,
    budget_left: Callable[[], int] | None = None,
) -> T:
    """Await `call` until it returns, or raise `Unavailable` when the attempts run out.

    `sleep` is a parameter for the same reason day 1's `retry` took one: a test should watch the
    schedule rather than live through it.

    `budget_left` is the one this desk needed and day 1 did not have. A retry is another model call
    and day 11's plugin will charge for it, so a retry that the budget cannot pay for must not be
    attempted — otherwise the desk spends its allowance discovering that it has none.
    """
    import asyncio

    waiter = sleep or asyncio.sleep
    last: Exception | None = None
    made = 0
    for attempt in range(1, attempts + 1):
        made = attempt
        try:
            return await call()
        except Transient as failure:
            last = failure
            if attempt == attempts:
                break
            if budget_left is not None and budget_left() <= 0:
                log("reliability.no_budget_to_retry", what=what, attempt=attempt)
                break
            wait = wait_for(attempt, failure)
            log(
                "reliability.retrying",
                what=what,
                attempt=attempt,
                of=attempts,
                waiting=wait,
                asked_for=failure.retry_after,
                detail=str(failure)[:120],
            )
            await waiter(wait)
    # `made`, not `attempts`: a run stopped by the budget guard made one attempt, and a log line
    # claiming three would send somebody looking for two failures that never happened.
    log("reliability.unavailable", what=what, attempts=made, allowed=attempts,
        detail=str(last)[:120])
    raise Unavailable(what, made, last or RuntimeError("no failure recorded"))


def idempotency_key(claim_id: str, outcome: str, reason: str | None, letter: str | None) -> str:
    """A name for one intended write, the same every time the same write is intended.

    Derived from the write's own content rather than from a counter or a clock: two attempts at the
    same decision produce the same key, and a *different* decision about the same claim produces a
    different one. That second half is what stops the key hiding a real change.

    Sixteen hex characters of SHA-256. Not a security boundary — nothing here is defending against
    an attacker choosing inputs — just a short, stable name that fits in a log line.
    """
    payload = json.dumps(
        {"claim_id": claim_id, "outcome": outcome, "reason": reason, "letter": letter},
        sort_keys=True,
    )
    return hashlib.sha256(payload.encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()[:16]


def already_written(existing: dict[str, Any] | None, key: str) -> bool:
    """Has this exact write already landed? Read the stored record, not a local cache.

    A cache of keys this process has seen would answer for this process. The question a retry
    actually asks is whether the *store* has it, and after a restart those are different answers.
    """
    return bool(existing) and existing.get("idempotency_key") == key
```

**Line by line:**

- **The docstring opens on day 1**, fourteen days back, and quotes the sentence that module was
  written for. This file is not new policy; it is the enforcement of policy that was already there.
- **"There is no fallback model in here and that is deliberate."** Said in the file rather than left
  as an absence, because a reliability module without a fallback chain looks unfinished. Part 2.1 is
  the argument.
- `ATTEMPTS = 3` **with the reasoning in the comment** — what the second attempt buys, what the
  third buys, and what a fourth does not. A constant with a number and no reasoning is a constant
  somebody doubles during an incident.
- `WAITS = tuple(delays(ATTEMPTS, base=1.0))` — **day 1's generator, called rather than
  reimplemented.** The comment says why: a project with two backoff curves has one nobody has
  measured.
- `LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT = 30.0` — **the cap, with the distinction that justifies it**: past this,
  "wait" stops being an instruction and becomes a refusal.
- `class Unavailable(RuntimeError)` **whose docstring is an instruction to the caller**: escalate,
  never substitute. The exception type is where that rule is most likely to be read.
- `def __init__(self, what, attempts, last)` — **three attributes kept, not just a message.** A
  caller that wants to log the underlying failure should not have to parse a string, and part 3.1
  asserts on `.attempts` and `.what` rather than on prose.
- `def wait_for(attempt, failure)` — **six lines holding the whole waiting policy**, extracted so a
  test can ask it questions without running a retry. Every branch in it is one sentence of the
  section above.
- `min(failure.retry_after, LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT)` — **the provider wins, up to the cap**, in one
  expression, in the order that reads as the rule.
- `WAITS[min(attempt - 1, len(WAITS) - 1)]` — **defensive index.** `attempts` is a parameter, the
  schedule is one shorter, and a caller passing a larger `attempts` should get the last wait
  repeated rather than an `IndexError` in the middle of an outage.
- `async def with_retry[T](what, call, *, attempts, sleep, budget_left)` — **generic over what the
  call returns**, so the retry does not care whether it is wrapping a triage, a letter or a tool.
- `what: str` **as the first argument, not an afterthought** — it goes in every log line this
  function writes, and "triage FNOL-4477" in a log is the difference between a diagnosable outage
  and three anonymous retry lines.
- `sleep: Callable[[float], Awaitable[None]] | None = None` — **a parameter for the same reason day
  1's took one**: a test should watch the schedule rather than live through it. Every test in part
  3.1 passes one and not one of them sleeps.
- `import asyncio` **inside the function** — the only module-level thing this file needs from
  asyncio is the default sleep, and importing it lazily keeps the module importable by anything that
  only wants `idempotency_key`.
- `except Transient as failure:` — **only day 1's exception is retried.** Anything else is a defect,
  and retrying a defect three times is three defects and a slower failure. Part 3.1 asserts a
  `ValueError` is tried exactly once.
- `if attempt == attempts: break` **before the budget check** — the last attempt is not followed by
  a wait, which is day 1's `delays` being one shorter than `attempts`, restated as control flow.
- `if budget_left is not None and budget_left() <= 0:` — **a callable, not a number.** The budget is
  being spent by the very calls this loop is making, so a value read once at the start would be
  stale by the second attempt.
- `log("reliability.retrying", ..., asked_for=failure.retry_after, ...)` — **both the wait taken and
  what the provider asked for.** They differ when the cap applies, and a log line showing only one
  of them cannot tell you which happened.
- `made = attempt` **tracked separately from `attempts`** — and the comment says why: a run stopped
  by the budget guard made one attempt, and a line claiming three sends somebody looking for two
  failures that never happened. This was a real defect in the first version of this file, caught by
  reading its own output.
- `def idempotency_key(...)` — part 1.2's subject, and it lives here rather than in the boundary
  because the desk decides what one write is.
- `def already_written(existing, key)` — **reads the stored record, not a cache.** The docstring
  gives the reason and it is the sharpest sentence in the file: after a restart, "has this process
  seen it" and "has the store got it" are different questions.

The waiting policy, asked directly:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from claims_desk import reliability
from claims_desk.util.backoff import Transient

print('attempts               :', reliability.ATTEMPTS)
print('waits when silent      :', reliability.WAITS)
print('longest honoured wait  :', reliability.LONGEST_HONOURED_WAIT)
for asked in (None, 0.5, 5, 900):
    print('  provider asks %-5s -> waits %s' % (
        asked, reliability.wait_for(1, Transient('busy', retry_after=asked))))
"
```

```
attempts               : 3
waits when silent      : (1.0, 2.0)
longest honoured wait  : 30.0
  provider asks None  -> waits 1.0
  provider asks 0.5   -> waits 0.5
  provider asks 5     -> waits 5
  provider asks 900   -> waits 30.0
```

**Line by line:**

- **Two waits for three attempts.** Day 1's `delays` yields one fewer than `attempts`, because the
  last attempt is followed by the exception rather than by a wait.
- `provider asks 0.5 -> waits 0.5` — **shorter than our schedule, and honoured.** The rule is not
  "wait at least as long as we planned"; it is that the provider decides.
- `provider asks 5 -> waits 5` — five times our first wait, honoured without argument.
- `provider asks 900 -> waits 30.0` — **the cap.** Fifteen minutes is a refusal wearing a number,
  and the desk escalates rather than holding a claim for it.

Now a busy provider that comes back. The stand-in refuses twice with a `Retry-After` of half a
second, then answers:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import asyncio, json
from claims_desk import reliability, sessions
from claims_desk.agents import classifier
from claims_desk.scripted import ScriptedTransient

waited = []
async def watch(seconds):
    waited.append(seconds)

async def main():
    model = ScriptedTransient(fail_times=2, retry_after=0.5)

    async def ask():
        agent = classifier.build(model=model, output_key='reading')
        said, _ = await sessions.turn(agent, 'FNOL-4471', 'the flexible hose let go overnight')
        return said

    said = await reliability.with_retry('triage FNOL-4471', ask, sleep=watch)
    print('answered  :', json.loads(said)['loss_type'])
    print('waited    :', waited)

asyncio.run(main())
"
```

```
{"event": "reliability.retrying", "what": "triage FNOL-4471", "attempt": 1, "of": 3, "waiting": 0.5, "asked_for": 0.5, "detail": "the provider was busy"}
{"event": "reliability.retrying", "what": "triage FNOL-4471", "attempt": 2, "of": 3, "waiting": 0.5, "asked_for": 0.5, "detail": "the provider was busy"}
answered  : escape-of-water
waited    : [0.5, 0.5]
```

**Line by line:**

- **Two retry lines and then an answer.** The third attempt succeeded, so there is no third line —
  the log records waits, not attempts, and the absence of a line is the success.
- `"waiting": 0.5, "asked_for": 0.5` — **identical here**, which is what you want to see: the
  provider asked and was obeyed. When the cap applies these two differ, and that difference is the
  only evidence the cap fired.
- `"attempt": 1, "of": 3` — **both numbers**, so a reader knows how much rope is left without
  looking up a constant.
- `waited: [0.5, 0.5]` — **the same wait twice, not 0.5 then 1.0.** The provider's interval is
  honoured on every attempt; the exponential schedule is only what happens when it says nothing.
- **The answer is the classifier's real one.** `ScriptedTransient` falls through to
  `ScriptedClassifier` once it stops failing, so what this proves is that a recovered retry produces
  the same answer as a first-attempt success, not a degraded one.

And when it does not come back:

```
{"event": "reliability.retrying", "what": "triage FNOL-4477", "attempt": 1, "of": 3, "waiting": 2, "asked_for": 2, "detail": "the provider was unreachable"}
{"event": "reliability.retrying", "what": "triage FNOL-4477", "attempt": 2, "of": 3, "waiting": 2, "asked_for": 2, "detail": "the provider was unreachable"}
{"event": "reliability.unavailable", "what": "triage FNOL-4477", "attempts": 3, "allowed": 3, "detail": "the provider was unreachable"}
Unavailable: triage FNOL-4477 failed after 3 attempts: the provider was unreachable
```

**Line by line:**

- **Three lines for three attempts**, the last one a different event. `reliability.unavailable` is
  the one worth alerting on; `reliability.retrying` is the one worth counting.
- `"attempts": 3, "allowed": 3` — **what was done and what was permitted.** They are equal here and
  the next demonstration is where they are not.
- **The exception carries the claim reference**, because `what` was `"triage FNOL-4477"`. A caller
  logging only `str(gone)` still has enough to find the claim.
- **Nothing was returned.** No default classification, no "assume no injury", no cached answer from
  a similar claim. The function raises and the desk escalates, which is day 1's sentence arriving
  where it was always going.

## When it breaks

Give the retry a budget with nothing in it — a claim whose earlier calls have used the allowance:

```
{"event": "reliability.no_budget_to_retry", "what": "triage FNOL-4477", "attempt": 1}
{"event": "reliability.unavailable", "what": "triage FNOL-4477", "attempts": 1, "allowed": 3, "detail": "the provider was busy"}
attempts made: 1 | triage FNOL-4477 failed after 1 attempts: the provider was busy
```

Verbatim, from this project. **One attempt, no waits, and an immediate escalation** — which is the
right behaviour and looks like a bug in a log until you read the first line.

That first line is the whole point of the guard. Without it, this claim makes three attempts, each
one charged by day 11's plugin, and the first two are refused by the budget anyway — so the desk
spends nothing useful, waits three seconds doing it, and arrives at the same escalation. Worse, on a
day when the provider is slow rather than down, those calls succeed and the *next* claim in the queue
finds an allowance that a retry has quietly eaten.

Now the part that was a real defect rather than a design. The first version of this file logged
`"attempts": attempts` on the `unavailable` line — the constant, not the count — so this exact run
printed `"attempts": 3` after making one. Nothing failed. No test caught it, because no test read
the log. It was found by running the demonstration for this part and noticing the number disagreed
with the demonstration's own output.

The fix is `made`, tracked in the loop, and `allowed` alongside it so both facts are on the line. The
lesson is narrower and more useful than "log carefully": **a log line that reports a constant instead
of a measurement is indistinguishable from a correct one**, and the only thing that finds it is
somebody reading the output next to what actually happened.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** The same shape, with a **circuit breaker** in front of it: after
some number of consecutive `Unavailable`s, stop attempting at all for a while and escalate
immediately. Three attempts per claim across a queue of several hundred is several hundred pointless
calls at a provider that is already struggling, and every one of them is a small act of
inconsiderateness that adds up to a slower recovery for everybody.

**What degrades at scale.** The waits are the problem, not the attempts. Twelve claims, each waiting
1 and then 2 seconds before escalating, adds 36 seconds to a queue that takes about 42. At a
morning's several hundred notifications a full outage turns a three-minute queue into a
twenty-minute one, spent almost entirely asleep — and the answer at the end is the same escalation it
would have reached immediately. That is the number that justifies the circuit breaker, and it is why
`Unavailable` carries `what`: the first thing a breaker needs is to count failures per dependency.

**The review comment.** *"`with_retry` wraps the whole triage, so a `Transient` from the boundary
retries the model call too. Today they are the same call so it does not matter. The moment triage
does two things, this retries the successful one — wrap the call that can fail, not the unit of
work."*

**The interview question.** *"A provider returns 429 with `Retry-After: 900`. Your retry policy says
1, 2, 4 seconds. What do you do, and what do you do differently if it says `Retry-After: 2`?"*
