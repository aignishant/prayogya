## One-line answer

A health check exists to answer one question — *should this process be sent traffic, or restarted?*
— so it may only test things a restart can fix, and the moment it calls the provider it starts
answering a different question and converting somebody else's bad minute into your outage.

## The idea

A lifeboat drill is not the emergency. It is the ship finding out, on a calm morning in harbour,
whether the davits still turn. The crew muster, the boat swings out, the falls run, and somebody
with a clipboard writes down what happened. Nobody is rescued. The entire value of the exercise is
that it produces evidence, on a day when the evidence is cheap, about equipment that will otherwise
only be tested on the day it matters.

Now imagine a drill card that ends with a line the mate cannot tick without the harbour tug. The tug
is late — it is often late, it belongs to somebody else — so the drill is recorded as failed. A
failed drill has a consequence written into the ship's standing orders: the vessel is held. So the
ship sits at the quay, fully seaworthy, with a boat that swings out perfectly, because a piece of
equipment that is not hers and that she cannot repair did not turn up. The card was wrong. Not the
ship.

That is exactly what a **health check** is, and exactly how one goes wrong. A health check is an
endpoint your own process answers — here, `GET /healthz` — that some other program calls on a
schedule to decide what to do with the process. In a container orchestrator that other program is
called a probe, and it has one lever: if the check fails often enough, it kills the process and
starts a new one. That lever is the whole context. **The question the caller is asking is not "is
everything well?" It is "should I restart you?"**

Once you see the question that way, the rule falls out of it. A restart replaces this process with a
fresh copy of the same code, on the same machine, with the same configuration. So a restart can fix
a wedged event loop, a leaked file handle, a module that half-imported, an in-process cache that has
gone bad. It cannot fix the provider being slow. It cannot fix a network partition. It cannot fix a
secret that was never injected into the environment. **A health check must not depend on anything a
restart cannot fix**, because a check that reports red on something a restart cannot fix produces
one behaviour and one only: an infinite loop of killing healthy processes.

Consider the version everyone writes first, the one that feels more thorough: `/healthz` asks the
model a one-word question and returns `ok` if the answer comes back. It looks better. It is strictly
worse, and it is worse in a way that only shows up on the day you least want it to. The provider has
a bad minute — a blip, the sort of thing that resolves itself. Every replica's check goes red at the
same instant, because they all depend on the same external thing. The orchestrator does the only
thing it knows how to do and kills all of them. Now the blip is over and you have no processes: they
are all in the middle of starting up, and their first act on startup will be to call the same
provider, at once. You did that. The provider's bad minute was survivable; the check turned it into
your outage, and it did so at whatever rate your probe is configured to run.

There is one more distinction the endpoint has to get right, and it is the tug again. This desk
needs an API key to answer a question. A missing key is a real problem and somebody should be woken
for it — but it is a **broken deployment**, not a broken process, and restarting will not produce a
key. So the key appears in the response as a **fact**, `key_wired`, reported and never used to
decide the verdict. Somebody reading the body learns immediately why `/ask` is failing. The probe
learns nothing, which is correct, because there is nothing here it can act on.

Two names, because they are two jobs. **FastAPI** is the framework that turns Python functions into
HTTP routes and validates what arrives; **uvicorn** is the server that actually holds the socket and
speaks HTTP. FastAPI cannot serve anything on its own and uvicorn knows nothing about your routes.
Both are pinned with `==` for the reason day 3 gave and this project's own `pins` check enforces: a
floor here turns `run.py check` red. `fastapi==0.141.1` and `uvicorn==0.52.4` were the current
releases on `https://pypi.org/pypi/fastapi/json` and `https://pypi.org/pypi/uvicorn/json`, read on
2026-09-10, and both now have dated rows in this project's `PACKAGES.md`.

`projects/01-ask-desk/ask_desk/api.py`, whole file — new today, and the first file in this project
that anything outside your own shell can reach:

```python
# ask_desk/api.py
"""The D1 surface: the desk behind HTTP, and a health endpoint that means something.

Two endpoints, and the small one is the one worth arguing about.

`/healthz` answers the question an orchestrator actually asks, which is **"should I send this
process traffic, or restart it?"** — not "is everything downstream well?". So it checks only what
this process can fix by restarting: that the module graph imported, that the agent was built, and
that the model it will call is one this project pinned. It does **not** call the provider, and it
does not read a key.

That restraint is the whole lesson. A health check that calls the model is a health check that goes
red when the provider has a bad minute, and the orchestrator responds by killing a process that was
working perfectly and starting a new one that will also fail — turning somebody else's blip into
your outage, and doing it at whatever rate your liveness probe runs. The rule: **a health check
must not depend on anything a restart cannot fix.**

`/ask` is the real work and needs a key. Its failures are reported as failures — a provider refusal
comes back as a 502 with the provider's own message, never as a cheerful answer with the error
swallowed.

Verified against fastapi 0.141.1 and uvicorn 0.52.4 on 2026-09-10.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fastapi import FastAPI, HTTPException
from google.genai import errors as genai_errors
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field

from ask_desk import agent, loop
from ask_desk.util import keys, models

app = FastAPI(title="Ask Desk", version="0.1.0")


class Question(BaseModel):
    """One question, and the session it belongs to."""

    question: str = Field(min_length=1, max_length=2000)
    session_id: str = "s-1"
    user_id: str = "local"


class Answer(BaseModel):
    """The answer, and the two numbers a caller needs to reason about cost."""

    answer: str
    model: str
    max_calls_per_question: int


@app.get("/healthz")
def healthz() -> dict[str, object]:
    """Liveness and readiness for this process only. No network, no key, no model call."""
    return {
        "status": "ok",
        "agent": agent.desk.name,
        # The pinned ID, read back through the registry, so a model this project never wrote down
        # cannot reach production behind a green health check.
        "model": models.require_pinned(agent.desk.model),
        "max_calls_per_question": loop.MAX_CALLS_PER_QUESTION,
        # Whether the key is *wired*, which is all a local check can decide (P00 day 3 part 1.2).
        # Reported as a fact, never as a reason to fail: a missing key is a broken deployment, and
        # restarting the process will not produce one.
        "key_wired": keys.get("GOOGLE_API_KEY") is not None,
    }


@app.post("/ask", response_model=Answer)
async def ask(request: Question) -> Answer:
    """Answer one question. Needs a working key; failures surface as failures."""
    try:
        answer = await agent.ask(
            request.question, user_id=request.user_id, session_id=request.session_id)
    except keys.MissingKey as exc:
        # 503, not 500: the process is fine and the deployment is not. The distinction is what
        # tells an operator whether to look at the code or at the environment.
        raise HTTPException(status_code=503, detail=str(exc)) from exc
    except genai_errors.ClientError as exc:
        raise HTTPException(status_code=502, detail=f"the provider refused this request: {exc}"
                            ) from exc
    return Answer(
        answer=answer,
        model=str(agent.desk.model),
        max_calls_per_question=loop.MAX_CALLS_PER_QUESTION,
    )
```

**Line by line:**
- **Four packages, not two, and the two extra ones are the point.** `fastapi` and `uvicorn` are
  obvious: this day introduces an HTTP surface. `pydantic` and `google-genai` were already
  installed before today — `google-adk` pulls both in — so `api.py` would have imported them
  happily with neither named here. Declaring them is the difference between *a package this
  project depends on* and *a package that happens to be present because something else wanted it*.
  The day `google-adk` drops or repins either one, the undeclared version breaks at import time in
  a file that never mentioned the dependency, and the traceback points at your code rather than at
  the change that caused it. **The rule: if a file in this project writes `import x`, `x` is in
  `pyproject.toml`.** Being installed is not the same claim as being depended on.


- The module docstring carries the argument, not just a description. That is deliberate and it is
  the pattern every file in this project follows: the reason a decision was made has to live beside
  the decision, because the next person to touch this file will be someone who wants `/healthz` to
  be "more thorough" and the docstring is the only thing standing in their way at that moment.
- `from fastapi import FastAPI, HTTPException` — two names, and `HTTPException` is the one that
  matters. It is how a handler says *this request failed, with this status* without the framework
  guessing. Raising anything else gets you a 500, which is the framework's honest way of saying it
  has no idea; part 1.2 is about why 500 is the wrong answer three times over here.
- `from google.genai import errors as genai_errors` — the provider SDK's exception module, imported
  under a name that says whose errors these are. `genai_errors.ClientError` is the class this
  project has actually seen: day 9's transcripts and this project's own `run.py adk` produce
  `google.genai.errors.ClientError: 400 INVALID_ARGUMENT` against a key the provider rejects. The
  aliased import is not cosmetic — `errors` alone, at module scope, is a name that will collide with
  something eventually, and the collision will be silent.
- `from pydantic import BaseModel, Field` — pydantic is FastAPI's schema layer. A class here is not
  a data holder; it is a **contract with the outside world**, and FastAPI reads it to decide what a
  valid request looks like before your function is ever entered.
- `from ask_desk import agent, loop` and `from ask_desk.util import keys, models` — every import in
  this file is either a third-party package this project pins or a module this project printed. That
  is the completeness rule showing up as an import list, and it is checkable by eye.
- `app = FastAPI(title="Ask Desk", version="0.1.0")` — the application object, created once at module
  scope. Module scope and not inside a function, because `uvicorn.run(api.app, ...)` in `run.py`
  needs an object to hand the server, and because the routes below are registered by decorators that
  run at import time.
- `question: str = Field(min_length=1, max_length=2000)` — the single most valuable line in the file
  for anything facing a network. `min_length=1` means an empty question is rejected **before**
  `ask()` is entered, so no session is created, no runner is built and no provider call is made for a
  request that was never going to produce an answer. `max_length=2000` is a bound on what a stranger
  can make you pay for: without it, one request can carry a payload large enough to be a cost
  incident on its own. Both are enforced by pydantic, which means the rejection is a 422 with a
  machine-readable body and none of your code ran.
- `session_id: str = "s-1"` and `user_id: str = "local"` with defaults — a caller who does not care
  about sessions gets a working request, and a caller who does can address one. Defaults on a request
  model are how you add a field later without breaking every existing client.
- `class Answer(BaseModel)` with `answer`, `model` and `max_calls_per_question` — the response says
  *which model produced this* and *what the ceiling on it was*. Both are facts a caller cannot
  otherwise discover, and both are the difference between an answer and an answer you can reason
  about. The bound is this project's own `MAX_CALLS_PER_QUESTION`, which day 4 chose and day 6 wired
  into `run_config`; it is reported rather than assumed.
- `@app.get("/healthz")` — a `GET`, because it reads and changes nothing, and the name with the `z`
  is the widely used convention that keeps an operational endpoint from colliding with a real route
  called `/health` in somebody's product. It takes no arguments and no body.
- `def healthz()` and **not** `async def healthz()` — this is worth stopping on. The body does no
  I/O: it reads three attributes and one dictionary. A plain `def` handler is run by FastAPI in a
  worker thread, so a slow one cannot block the event loop; an `async def` handler that did anything
  blocking would block every other request in the process. Since this function is deliberately the
  one function in the file with nothing to await, `def` says so.
- `"status": "ok"` as a literal — there is no branch that produces anything else. That reads as
  laziness and is the opposite: **every failure mode of this endpoint is an exception**, which
  becomes a non-200, which is what the probe reads. A check that returns `200 {"status": "bad"}`
  has told the machine everything is fine, and part 2.2's whole subject is what that costs.
- `"agent": agent.desk.name` — touching `agent.desk` forces the import of `ask_desk.agent`, which
  builds the `Agent`, which resolves its tools. If any of that is broken, the request raises here
  rather than at the first real question. That is the endpoint doing its actual job: proving the
  module graph imported and the agent was constructible, both of which a restart can fix.
- `"model": models.require_pinned(agent.desk.model)` — not `str(agent.desk.model)`. The registry from
  day 4 is asked to confirm the ID again, on every check. If someone edits the agent to a `-latest`
  alias, or to a model this project never wrote down, `require_pinned` raises `UnpinnedModel` and the
  endpoint stops being 200. This is the line that makes it impossible to ship an unpinned model
  behind a green health check, and it costs nothing because the registry is a dictionary in memory.
- `"max_calls_per_question": loop.MAX_CALLS_PER_QUESTION` — the bound, published. An operator
  looking at a cost graph can read the ceiling out of the deployment instead of out of the source.
- `"key_wired": keys.get("GOOGLE_API_KEY") is not None` — `get`, never `require`. `require` raises;
  `get` returns `None`. The difference is the whole design: this is a **fact in the body**, and the
  status code does not move because of it. A missing key is a broken deployment and a restart will
  not produce one, so failing here would produce an endless restart loop and no key at the end of
  it. Note also what it does *not* claim: `key_wired: true` means a value is present, not that the
  provider will accept it, which is the third state of a key that P00 day 3 part 1.2 proved cannot
  be decided locally.
- `@app.post("/ask", response_model=Answer)` — a `POST`, because it does work and costs money, and
  because the question belongs in a body rather than in a URL that lands in every access log.
  `response_model=Answer` makes the shape of the reply part of the route rather than part of the
  return statement.
- `async def ask(...)` — this one **is** async, and it has to be: it awaits `agent.ask`, which awaits
  the runner, which is doing network I/O. Written as a plain `def`, the `await` would be a syntax
  error; written as `async def` with a blocking call inside, it would freeze every other request.
- `except keys.MissingKey` **before** `except genai_errors.ClientError` — the order matters because
  the first matching clause wins, and these are two different diagnoses. `agent.ask` calls
  `keys.require` before it builds anything, so a deployment with no key never reaches the provider
  at all, and the operator should be told that rather than being shown a provider error.
- `raise HTTPException(status_code=503, ...) from exc` — the `from exc` chains the original
  exception, so the traceback in your logs still names `keys.py` and the line that raised. Drop
  `from exc` and you get `During handling of the above exception, another exception occurred`, which
  is noise, or a clean traceback pointing at `api.py` and telling you nothing.
- `detail=str(exc)` — the `MissingKey` message names the key and the file it belongs in, which day 4
  wrote it to do. It is safe to return because it contains no secret; it contains the *name* of one.
- `detail=f"the provider refused this request: {exc}"` — the provider's own message, prefixed with
  whose message it is. An operator reading `502 the provider refused this request: 400
  INVALID_ARGUMENT ... API key not valid` needs no further investigation to know which half of the
  system to look at.
- `return Answer(...)` — constructed explicitly rather than returning a dict. If a field is missing
  or the wrong type, it fails here, in your process, at the line that built it, rather than being
  serialised into a reply some client will fail to parse tomorrow.

The endpoint, over real HTTP. The server is started in one shell with
`uv run --frozen python run.py serve`, and this is another:

```console
$ curl -s http://127.0.0.1:8080/healthz
{"status":"ok","agent":"ask_desk","model":"gemini-3.8-flash","max_calls_per_question":6,"key_wired":true}
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://127.0.0.1:8080/healthz
200
```

*Observed on this machine on 2026-09-10, fastapi 0.141.1, uvicorn 0.52.4, google-adk 2.8.0.*

**Line by line:**

- `"model":"gemini-3.8-flash"` came back through `require_pinned`, so this is not the agent reporting
  what it was configured with — it is the registry confirming that what the agent was configured with
  is a pin this project wrote down.
- `"max_calls_per_question":6` is this project's own constant, not the framework's default of 500.
  The number in the response is the number a question can actually cost.
- `"key_wired":true` on a machine whose key is the synthetic one from `.env`. That is the endpoint
  being honest about the limits of what it can know: the value is present, and the last two days of
  transcripts show the provider rejecting it. Wired is not valid, and this field claims only the
  first.
- The second `curl` exists because the first one does not tell you the status. `-o /dev/null -w
  "%{http_code}\n"` throws the body away and prints the number, which is the only part the probe
  reads. This is day 3's lesson wearing an HTTP hat: **the body is decoration, the status is the
  verdict.**
- **No provider call was made to answer either request.** That is checkable and worth checking: this
  project's only path to the network is `provider.py` and the framework's client, and nothing in
  `healthz` reaches either.

## When it breaks

The version that calls the model does not fail the way you expect. It does not time out; it raises,
from inside a library, about a place you did not think you were configuring. This is the real text,
observed on this machine before `agent.export_key()` existed, from a call that reached the framework
with the key sitting on disk in `.env` the whole time:

```console
ValueError: No API key was provided. Please pass a valid API key. Learn how to create an API key at https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/api-key.
```

Somebody shipped a `/healthz` that "verified the model connection" on a Friday. At 11pm the probe
started failing on every replica at once, each one 500ing with that message, and the on-call engineer
spent an hour proving the key was present — because it was, in the file, exactly where the runbook
said to put it. The check was not testing the process; it was testing a library's view of an
environment variable, which is a thing no restart has ever fixed. The smallest fix is the file above:
delete the call, report `key_wired` as a fact, and let the endpoint answer only the question the
probe is entitled to ask.

## In production

**What a senior writes instead.** Two endpoints, not one. `/healthz` stays exactly this shape — a
liveness probe, cheap, dependency-free, answering *is this process wedged*. Alongside it goes
`/readyz`, a readiness probe, which is allowed to check things this process needs before it can take
traffic and whose failure removes the pod from the load balancer **without killing it**. That is the
distinction the two-endpoint split buys: readiness is reversible, liveness is a death sentence. If
you only ever write one, write this one, because a wrong readiness check costs you traffic and a
wrong liveness check costs you the process.

**What degrades at scale.** Three replicas with a liveness probe running every ten seconds is 1,080
requests an hour. If the check calls the model, that is 1,080 provider requests an hour that answer
nobody's question, before a single user has asked anything — and every one of them is a request you
cannot afford on a free tier whose ceiling is not even published (see this project's `PACKAGES.md`).
Worse than the volume is the correlation: all three replicas depend on the same external service, so
they do not fail independently, they fail together, and the orchestrator's response to all three
failing is to have zero.

**The review comment.** "This health check calls the provider. When the provider has a bad minute
every replica goes red simultaneously and the orchestrator kills all of them, so a partial upstream
degradation becomes a total local outage — please make `/healthz` process-local and put the
dependency check in a separate `/readyz` that removes the pod from the pool instead of restarting
it. Also, `key_wired` should stay a field and never a status: a restart cannot produce a secret."

**The interview question.** "Your service's liveness probe checks that it can reach the database. The
database has a thirty-second blip. Walk me through what your cluster does in the following minute,
and then tell me what the check should have been instead — and what you would have lost by making
that change."
